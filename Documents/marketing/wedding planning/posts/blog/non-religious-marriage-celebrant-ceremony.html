No god, no script, no awkward silence: what a non-religious celebrant actually writes

Topics in this guide

couplescelebrantsStage: Celebrant & suppliersAustraliaauthenticityconfidence

Most weddings in Australia are no longer held in a church. Australian Bureau of Statistics figures show civil celebrants conducting roughly three-quarters of marriages in recent years, and in the 2021 Census about 39 per cent of people said they had no religion. If you search for a non-religious marriage celebrant near you, you are joining the majority. Yet many couples still approach the ceremony with a quiet worry: if there is no hymn, no prayer and no minister, what exactly happens for twenty minutes?

The answer is that a good celebrant fills those minutes with you. This guide explains what is legally required, what is optional, how celebrants build a ceremony from scratch and how to search for the right one.

What "non-religious" means in practice

In Australia a non-religious ceremony is led by a civil marriage celebrant, someone registered by the Commonwealth to solemnise marriages without being tied to a church. Some celebrants also describe themselves as humanist or secular, which usually means they avoid religious language by default. The legal weight is identical to a religious wedding. The marriage is valid whether it happens in a garden, a pub or a cathedral.

What the law requires, and nothing more

The legal minimum is short. Everything else is a design choice.

  • A statement from the celebrant explaining what marriage is under Australian law.
  • A set of words from each of you taking the other as your spouse. The celebrant provides the form of words.
  • Two adult witnesses, and the signing of the marriage certificates.
  • A valid Notice of Intended Marriage lodged at least a month in advance. See the elopement guide for the full paperwork list.

That whole legal core takes a few minutes. The rest is ceremony, and it is where a celebrant earns their fee.

What a typical secular ceremony looks like

There is no required order, but most celebrants use a variation of this.

  1. Welcome. The celebrant welcomes guests and sets the tone. Many Australian ceremonies begin with an acknowledgement of Country, which is optional and worth discussing.
  2. Your story. A short account of how you met and what you value in each other. This is the part guests remember.
  3. A reading or two. A poem, a passage from a novel, song lyrics or a letter from someone who could not be there. Friends and family often read.
  4. The legal statement. The celebrant's formal words about marriage.
  5. Vows. Personal vows, if you want them, followed by the required legal words.
  6. Rings, or another symbol. Rings are traditional. Some couples exchange something else, or add a ritual.
  7. The declaration and the kiss. The celebrant pronounces you married.
  8. Signing the register. Often done at a small table with witnesses, sometimes to music.
  9. The exit. The recessional, with the first photographs close behind.

A full ceremony usually runs somewhere between fifteen and thirty minutes, depending on readings and rituals.

Ways to make it feel full, not thin

  • Write personal vows. Keep them short. Two minutes each is enough, and a rough draft read aloud at home will tell you what works.
  • Give friends a job. A reading, a ring-bearer role or a blessing from a grandparent makes guests feel part of it.
  • Add a ritual with meaning, not just prettiness. Handfasting, a shared drink, planting a tree, blending two sands or oils, or each guest warming the rings in their hands as they pass them along. Choose one that connects to your story and skip the ones that feel borrowed.
  • Use music with care. A song for the entrance and one for the exit are plenty, with a quiet instrumental during the signing.
  • Leave a space for silence. A pause after the vows is not awkward. It is the point.
  • Include the people who are not there. A mention of absent grandparents or friends overseas, handled briefly and warmly, lands well.

Readings with no religious content

If you do not want scripture, the options are wide: poetry, essays, song lyrics, children's books, letters, a short passage from a favourite novel or a passage from an author who means something to both of you. Ask your celebrant for their collection, then choose something that sounds like you rather than something that sounds like a wedding.

If your families are religious

Many couples marry in a secular ceremony with religious family in the front row. A good celebrant can include a blessing, a reading or a short tradition from a family's culture without turning the ceremony into a religious service. Talk to the people involved in advance, be clear about what you will and will not include, and let your celebrant carry the conversation if it is delicate.

How to search for a celebrant near you

  • Start with the register. The Attorney-General's Department keeps the Register of Marriage Celebrants. Confirm registration before you pay a deposit.
  • Use your venue, photographer and friends. Venues often have preferred lists, and photographers see dozens of celebrants at work. A recommendation from someone who watched the ceremony is worth more than a star rating.
  • Read, then listen. Websites tell you about style. A phone or video call tells you about fit. Within ten minutes you will know whether you want this person at the front of your day.
  • "Near me" matters less than you think. A registered celebrant can marry you anywhere in Australia. Travel fees are common, and the best fit may be an hour away. Closeness helps for rehearsals and for meeting in person, but video calls handle most of the process.
  • Check the style. Some celebrants are traditional and structured. Others are relaxed, humorous or poetic. Read sample ceremonies, ask to see a script and look for someone whose voice you would enjoy hearing.

What to look out for

  • A template ceremony. If the sample scripts all sound identical, you may be buying a form, not a ceremony.
  • Unclear fees. Ask what is included: meetings, a draft script, a rehearsal, travel, a PA system, document lodgement and the commemorative certificate.
  • A celebrant who talks more than they listen. Your story should shape the ceremony, not the other way around.
  • Availability on your date. Popular celebrants book months ahead, especially for spring and autumn weekends.

The measure of a good ceremony

A secular ceremony succeeds when the guests could not imagine it belonging to anyone else. If they leave knowing something new about you and laughing at one small story, the lack of a hymn will never cross their minds.

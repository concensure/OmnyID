Wedding favours guests will actually take home

Topics in this guide

couplesguestsStage: Details & invitesconsiderationjoy

Picture the end of a reception. The band is packing up, the lights come up, and someone from the venue walks the room with a tub, collecting what's left on the tables. In the tub: a stack of personalised trinkets nobody picked up. On the cookie table, meanwhile, the take-home sleeves are gone and the platter has been picked clean.

That scene comes up again and again when couples compare notes on wedding favours. One bride in a planning community put it plainly: unless it's food or something genuinely useful, a lot of favours get left behind. Another said the only favours she'd ever seen vanish completely were a cookie table with sleeves to take home and scratch-off lottery tickets. We gathered the best of what couples shared, so you can spend your wedding favour budget on things that leave the room in a guest's pocket.

Why most favours end up in the tub

It's rarely that guests are ungrateful. It's that a favour has to survive the journey home. After a long day of travel, a few drinks and a late night, nobody wants to carry something fragile, bulky or stamped with a date that isn't theirs. If it needs a bag of its own, it tends to stay put.

The favours that do travel share three traits: they're small, they're consumable (eat it, use it up, no guilt), and they feel chosen rather than ordered in bulk.

Edible wedding favours that vanish first

Food is the safest bet, and the best versions are tied to a place or a person.

  • Something local. Mini jars of honey from a favourite local honey farmer, hand-made truffles from a nearby chocolatier, or a single indulgent treat from the bakery behind your favourite dessert. A guest who tastes it remembers where you're from.
  • Something you made. Little jars of homemade strawberry jam from a bride who loves canning. Mango jam from mangoes grown at home. Apple butter in tiny jars. It costs more in time than money, and nobody mistakes it for generic.
  • A tradition. Greek weddings give sugared almonds as a matter of course, often alongside traditional Greek desserts, and some couples add a jar of hot sauce for good measure. If your family has a version of this, use it. It carries meaning no catalogue item can.
  • A little personality. His-and-hers favourite lollies work well. So does a mix of white and pink chocolate candies in a bag with a sticker of your names and date. A pun helps too: one couple gave out mini mints with a tag reading "Allison and Collin are mint to be".
  • A dessert table that doubles as the favour. A cookie table with paper sleeves or boxes lets guests build their own take-home bag, and nothing goes to waste. Add a scratch-off ticket and you've covered the sweet tooth and the gambler.
A favour doesn't have to be a thing. It just has to be something a guest is glad to carry out of the room.

Useful favours people actually keep

If food isn't your style, aim for something that earns a place in a kitchen, a garden or a handbag.

  • Mini succulents with a thank-you note. A wedding coordinator reported that guests at a wedding she ran the day before loved the little succulents, each with a note attached. Plants came up repeatedly as a favourite. They look good on the table and then on a windowsill. If guests are travelling interstate or overseas, check biosecurity rules first, since plants and seeds are restricted in some places.
  • Bottle openers and stoppers. Cheap, small and used for years. Keychain openers in a bowl on the table are the lowest-effort version.
  • Coasters. Cork or acrylic, stamped to look like a vinyl record with your names and date, is a nice nod if music is your thing.
  • Seed packets. A pollinator mix weighs nothing and gives guests a reason to think of you in spring.

Or skip them, and say so with confidence

There's a respectable camp that says the best favour is a great night. "The free food and drinks," one person wrote. "Party favours are a waste of money, they leave them half the time." Another answered simply, "A nice time." One couple put out Hershey's Kisses on the tables and moved on.

If you'd rather not give a favour, you can redirect the money to a better bar tab, a late-night snack, or a donation in your guests' honour. Just decide on purpose, so it doesn't feel like something you forgot.

Practical things to settle early

  • Allergies and dietary needs. If you're gifting food, especially homemade, label ingredients clearly. Honey, nuts and baked goods are the usual flashpoints.
  • One per guest or one per household? For anything bulky, one per household keeps cost and carrying down.
  • Out-of-town guests. A welcome bag at their accommodation, with a snack, water and a printed schedule, often does more good than anything on the reception table.
  • The headcount. Favours go wrong when the guest list shifts. Order against your final confirmed numbers, plus a small buffer.

Keeping favours in the plan

Favours sound small until you're ordering 90 jars with a three-week lead time and a late RSVP changes the count.

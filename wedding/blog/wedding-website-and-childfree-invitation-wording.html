Wedding websites and kid-free invitations: what to say and where to say it

Topics in this guide

couplesguestsfamiliesStage: Details & invitesetiquette anxietyconfidence

A wedding website saves you from repeating the same answers in dozens of messages. An adults-only wedding is a reasonable choice, but the way you communicate it matters more than the rule itself. Both are mostly about being clear, early and consistent.

Choosing a website

There are many free and paid builders. Rather than recommending one, here is what to compare:

  • RSVP and guest management: can guests reply for named people only, record dietary needs and update later?
  • Privacy: can you password protect the site, and where is guest data stored? Your guests' addresses and dietary details are personal information.
  • Mobile first: most guests will open it on a phone.
  • Cost and ads: check whether the free version shows adverts or locks features, and what it costs to keep it live after the wedding.
  • Export: you should be able to download your guest list and replies.

What to put on it

  • Date, start time and the venue address, with a map pin and parking notes.
  • Dress code and weather notes, such as shade, heat or footwear for grass.
  • Accommodation and transport, including late-night options.
  • RSVP deadline and how to reply.
  • Registry or gift preferences, in a short and kind way.
  • An FAQ covering children, plus ones, drinks and photography.
  • If drinks are not fully hosted, say so. See drinks options explained.

Wording for an adults-only wedding

The invitation should address only the people invited, by name. Then repeat the policy in one more place, gently, such as on the website.

Sample lines for the website FAQ

  • "We love your little ones, but our wedding is an adults-only celebration. We hope this gives you a night off."
  • "Due to venue capacity, we are unable to include children, apart from those in our wedding party."
  • "We have arranged a list of local babysitters. Let us know if you would like it."

Wording to avoid

  • Lines that sound like a warning, such as "No children, no exceptions."
  • Putting the rule only on the invitation envelope without a reason.

Practical steps that help

  • Decide the age cut-off and the exceptions early. Common exceptions include nursing babies, children in the wedding party and family members. Apply them consistently.
  • Tell people before the save-the-date goes out if you are worried about reactions, especially close family.
  • Make it easy for out-of-town guests with childcare options, a later start or accommodation near the venue.
  • Expect that a few people may decline. That is their choice, and fine.

If a guest brings a child anyway

If it happens before the wedding, call the guest, thank them for replying and restate the policy kindly, with an offer to help find childcare. If a child arrives on the day, a quiet word from someone in your family or the venue is gentler than a scene. Whether to make an exception is yours to decide; the important thing is that you have not made it awkward for the guests who followed the rule.

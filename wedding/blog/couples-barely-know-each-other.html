You’ve been together for years. Do you know what your partner wants at the wedding?

Topics in this guide

couplespartnersStage: Plan & budgetcuriosityjoy

You know their coffee order. You know which side of the bed, which streaming show, which of your friends they secretly find exhausting. Then someone asks, “How many guests are you thinking?” and you each say a number out loud for the first time. They say 40. You said 140.

It is one of the most common moments in wedding planning, and it is nobody’s fault. A wedding is a new kind of decision. You have no shared history of making it, so you each quietly build a different wedding in your head and assume the other is looking at the same picture.

Three answers couples tend to discover late

  • The guest list. It drives the venue, the catering and most of the budget, yet it is often settled by assumption rather than conversation.
  • The number. Not the maximum you could spend. The amount you could open your wallet for without flinching. Two people can love each other and have very different answers.
  • The thing you would cut last. Flowers, food, music, photos, a particular person on the guest list. What hurts to lose tells you what you value, and it is rarely what you would say if asked directly.

None of these are hard questions. They are just rarely asked in the first month, when they are cheap to answer, and often asked in month six, when a deposit has been paid.

Why pictures work better than questions

Ask someone “what style is your wedding?” and you get a shrug and the word “elegant”. Show them six photographs and ask which one they would frame, and the answer is instant and honest: a cliff-top elopement, a chandelier ballroom, a string-light supper, a moody dance floor. People know what they feel before they have words for it.

That is the idea behind The Wedding Animal Quiz: twelve picture questions about originality, planning structure, guests, financial trade-offs, decision tempo and decision ownership. Your answers produce one of eight playful storytelling labels—Fox, Owl, Dolphin, Elephant, Peacock, Otter, Beaver or Swan—plus the evidence behind it.

What you actually get

The animals are the fun part. The useful part sits underneath. Your result gives you:

  • six visible preference scales, each showing how many answers support it;
  • a playful animal label and the literal choices behind it;
  • a practical next action, such as setting shortlist criteria or approval limits;
  • an optional partner comparison that celebrates one agreement and raises no more than two conversations;
  • reviewable suggestions for inspiration, reminders, guest planning, financial trade-offs, shortlist size and approval workflow.

The useful differences

The comparison focuses on planning behaviour rather than relationship judgement: how many options to explore, which deadlines need structure, how decisions should be owned and where cost certainty matters. If you differ, you have simply found a practical rule worth agreeing while it is still easy to change your mind.

What this is, and what it isn’t

It is a conversation starter built around decisions that are easy to assume and rarely said aloud. It is not a psychological test, we have not run a study on couples, and we are not going to quote you a statistic about how well couples know each other. The animals are a friendly way to start talking, not a verdict on your relationship.

Privacy, plainly

The quiz is free and needs no email address. Your answers stay on your device. When you send the link to your partner, your answers travel inside the link itself rather than to a server, so send it to them only. Nothing is saved to a workspace unless you choose to start planning.

From quiz to plan

If you want to carry on, OmnyID brings the supported planning preferences into onboarding as suggestions. It does not invent a budget, guest count, date or location, and it does not overwrite facts you already confirmed. You can keep, edit or remove each suggestion before it shapes the plan.

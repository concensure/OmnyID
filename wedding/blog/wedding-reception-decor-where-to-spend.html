Wedding reception decor: what guests photograph, and what quietly gets binned

Topics in this guide

couplesvenuesstylistsStage: Details & invitescreativityclarity

Open a stack of real wedding photographs and look at the reception pages. The ones that stay with you are rarely the ones with the most decoration. They are the ones with warm light, a table that looks like people are about to eat and a few well-placed flowers. The scattered table confetti, the paper pennant, the custom neon sign that cost a week's wages: those barely register.

Reception decor is one of the easiest budget lines to inflate, because every item looks small on its own. This guide is about deciding where the money shows, where it does not and how to avoid collecting a pile of beautiful things you pay to hire, carry and bin. It will help whether you are styling a ballroom, a barn or a backyard.

A quick audit for every item

Before you add anything to the plan, ask three questions:

  1. Will a guest notice it? If it is behind a speaker, under a table or only visible from the ceiling, it may not earn its cost.
  2. Will it be in a photograph? Photographers capture rings, place settings, the cake, the head table and the room as people walk in. Check with yours.
  3. Can it be reused or returned? Hired or resellable items cost less than one-use pieces.

If the answer to all three is no, take it off the list.

Lighting first

If you have one extra dollar to spend, spend it on light. Many venues are bright, harsh and flat by default. Warm string lights, lamps, candles in safe holders and soft uplighting along a wall change the whole feel of the room. Ask the venue what it can dim or adjust, whether you can hire lighting and who sets it up. An outdoor or industrial space may need festoon lighting and a power supply. Photographers also work better in soft, even light, so share your plan with them.

Flowers: concentrate, don't scatter

A few generous arrangements look more expensive than many small ones. Put the volume where people look: the ceremony backdrop, the entry, the head or sweetheart table and the cake. Seasonal and locally grown flowers are generally fresher and often cheaper, and foliage can fill space at a lower price. Our florist and DJ questions guide covers what to ask and what a quote should include.

Tables people actually sit at

A long table with linen, mismatched glassware, a few candles and a loose row of flowers or greenery gives the "dinner party" look many couples want, and costs less than a sea of identical centrepieces. A useful rule is to keep centrepieces either low, below eye level, or tall and slim, above it, so guests can see each other across the table. A middle-height arrangement blocks conversation and gets moved.

What quietly gets binned

  • Favours nobody takes. See our guide to favours guests actually keep.
  • Confetti, glitter and petals. Many venues ban them because of clean-up, and they create waste. Biodegradable options exist, but check the rules first.
  • Balloon arches and helium balloons. Some venues disallow them, and they deflate by the time of the photographs.
  • Over-themed props. A large monogram, a neon sign and a themed backdrop are fun, but add up. Choose one statement piece at most.
  • Centrepieces for every table at maximum height. Costly to produce, and they block faces.
  • Anything that needs rescuing on the day. If an item needs a ladder, a helper and an hour of setup, ask whether it earns its place.

Hire, buy or make it yourself

  • Hire large and bulky items you will use once: chairs, tables, linen, glassware, lighting and arches. It saves storage and removal, and the supplier takes responsibility for damage within the contract.
  • Buy small items that you will reuse or can resell, such as candle holders, table numbers or a sign. Bridal resale groups are active.
  • DIY only if you have the time and the skill. A handmade item can look charming, but a hundred identical ones take many hours. Put a value on your own time and the time of your helpers.
  • Borrow or thrift. Op shops and markets are full of glassware, vases and candlesticks that look good together because they do not match. It is also a growing style.
  • Use living things. Potted plants, herbs and small trees can decorate the room and then go home as gifts or garden additions.

The venue rules that catch people out

  • Tape, nails, blu-tack and adhesives on walls, ceilings and furniture.
  • Open flames, including candles, sparklers and lanterns, and what holders are allowed.
  • Confetti, glitter, rice and balloons.
  • Access times for deliveries and set-up, and when everything must be gone.
  • Power supply, extension leads and who can use them.
  • Who is responsible for packing down and returning hired items.

Ask the venue for these rules in writing, and share them with your florist, stylist and hire company. If you are using an unconventional space, our guide to the hidden costs of unusual venues explains why decor and infrastructure often come as a package.

Trends worth borrowing, and why

  • Dinner-party tables. Long tables, mixed glassware, linen napkins and candles. Relaxed, personal and photogenic. See also our Australian wedding trends overview.
  • Seasonal and local flowers. Often better value and lower environmental impact, and they look right for the time of year.
  • Candlelight and soft light. Lighting continues to be the most effective styling tool at any budget.
  • Personal touches. Framed photographs, family heirlooms, handwritten menus and a table with names that have meaning. They give the room a story.
  • Lower-waste styling. Dried and potted flowers, hired items and reusable decor, in place of single-use pieces.

Plan the details around the day

  • Set a clear style in a sentence. "Warm, candlelit dinner party" guides every choice, and keeps you from buying things that do not fit.
  • Choose a person to be in charge. A planner, stylist or trusted friend with a list and a timeline, so you are not arranging flowers in your dress.
  • Ask your photographer when they will photograph the room. The best time is after styling and before guests arrive. Make sure it is ready.
  • Plan the pack-down. Decide who takes the flowers, who returns the hire and who collects personal items. Label boxes and give people a list.

The best decor is invisible in the right way: guests remember how the room felt, not what was on the tables. Spend where it shows, skip where it does not and write everything down in one place, including quantities, suppliers and set-up times.

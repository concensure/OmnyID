Lab-grown diamonds are real diamonds. That does not make them a good buy.

Topics in this guide

couplesjewellersStage: Plan & budgetAustraliacost anxietyclarity

For a century, the diamond engagement ring came with an unspoken rule: the stone is rare, it takes millions of years to form and it keeps its value. Lab-grown diamonds have quietly knocked down every part of that story except one. They are real diamonds. They are chemically and physically the same as mined stones, they sparkle the same and they cost far less.

That has made the lab-grown diamond engagement ring one of the most-discussed purchases in wedding planning, and one of the most confusing. Retailers describe a fast-growing market and falling prices. Critics warn that a stone worth thousands on the day you buy it may be worth a fraction of that a year later. Both can be true. This guide sets out what you are buying, how to compare and where to be careful, so you can decide what matters to you.

What a lab-grown diamond is, and is not

A lab-grown diamond is a diamond grown in a factory, using one of two processes. In one, carbon is subjected to extreme pressure and temperature, copying the conditions underground. In the other, a thin slice of diamond is placed in a chamber, and carbon-rich gas builds a new crystal on it layer by layer. The result is carbon in a diamond crystal structure, the same as a mined stone. Gemmological laboratories can tell them apart with specialist equipment, but a jeweller's loupe will not.

It is not a "fake", a "synthetic" in the sense of an imitation, or the same thing as cubic zirconia or moissanite. Those are different materials that look like diamond. Lab-grown diamonds are diamond.

The price, and why it keeps changing

Lab-grown stones are significantly cheaper than mined stones of the same size and quality, and retail prices have kept falling as production has expanded. One Australian retailer's blog estimates that a 1.5 carat lab-grown solitaire ring typically costs around $2,500 to $6,000, against $12,000 to $20,000 or more for a natural stone of similar quality. These figures come from a seller, and there is no authoritative Australian price index, so treat them as a rough indication and ask for several quotes.

The same fall in price is the reason for the warning that follows.

Resale value: the part to understand before you buy

A mined diamond has a modest resale market, often well below what you paid at retail, but a market all the same. Lab-grown diamonds are a different case. Because they can be produced in large quantities and prices keep dropping, the resale value is low. Retailer blogs give estimates of 10 to 40 per cent of the original price, which vary, and trade press has reported a Sydney teacher whose lab-grown ring, bought for $9,000 in 2021, was later valued at about $500. Take those figures with caution, but the direction is clear.

The sensible conclusion is not that lab-grown is a poor choice. It is that you should buy a ring because you love it and can afford it, not because you expect it to hold value. If you want your money to be protected, get the ring valued and insure it, as you would any other piece of jewellery, rather than treating it as an investment. Some jewellers offer trade-in or upgrade programmes. If one does, get the terms in writing.

Grading and certificates

Lab-grown diamonds are graded on the same four Cs as mined stones: cut, colour, clarity and carat weight. Major gemmological laboratories, including GIA and IGI, issue reports for lab-grown stones. One retailer claims that stones from different labs can differ in consistency and resale appeal. We could not confirm that independently, so treat it as an opinion and compare reports yourself.

  • Insist on a report from a recognised lab. It should match the stone you are buying, including the report number.
  • Look for a laser inscription. Many lab-grown and mined stones carry a tiny number on the girdle that matches the report. Ask whether yours has one.
  • Make sure the invoice says "lab-grown". Australian consumer law requires sellers to describe products honestly, and the invoice should leave no doubt about what you bought.
  • Ask about treatments. Some stones are treated after growth to improve colour. A good seller will disclose it.
  • Prioritise cut. A well-cut stone sparkles far more than a larger one with a poor cut. A lab-grown stone allows you to put the savings into cut quality, not just carat weight.

The ethics question, honestly

Many couples choose lab-grown diamonds for ethical or environmental reasons. The picture is more complicated than either side's marketing. Mined diamonds have a history that includes conflict and unsafe labour, and an industry-wide certification scheme, the Kimberley Process, aims to keep conflict diamonds out of the market, though critics say it has limits. Lab-grown diamonds avoid mining, but they use a lot of energy, and their environmental footprint depends largely on whether that energy comes from renewable sources. Claims that one type is automatically "greener" or more ethical should be questioned.

If this matters to you, ask the seller specific questions. Where was the stone grown, and what energy powered the factory? For mined stones, can the seller trace the origin? Australia's Argyle mine, once famous for pink diamonds, closed in 2020, and that history is part of why provenance questions come up in this country. Vague statements such as "eco-friendly" should be a prompt to ask for details, not a reason to relax.

Where to find them

  • Independent jewellers. Many now offer both mined and lab-grown stones, and can talk through the options without a hard sell.
  • Online specialists. Often the lowest prices, with a wide choice and detailed reports. Check return policies, insurance, delivery and customs, and what happens if you want to resize.
  • Custom designers. Good for choosing a stone and a setting separately, which can give you better value.
  • Big chains. Easy to see and try, with standard warranties. Compare their prices with independents.

Questions to ask before you pay

  1. Is the stone lab-grown, how was it grown and is it treated?
  2. Which lab issued the report, and does the number match the stone?
  3. What are the cut, colour, clarity and carat weight, and what do they look like in daylight?
  4. What is the return or exchange policy, and is there a trade-in option?
  5. How is the setting made, and what are the metal and its purity?
  6. What does insurance and valuation cost, and who provides it?

Thoughtful additions

  • Talk to your partner about what matters. Some people feel strongly about tradition, others about cost or ethics. Better to know before the shop.
  • Decide what the savings are for. A lower price on the stone could mean a better setting, a stone with a better cut, or money for the honeymoon. Our budgeting guide shows how to put savings to work.
  • Think about the band. If you want matching metals, plan for the wedding bands at the same time. See our guide to men's wedding bands.
  • Include rings in the budget from the start. It is one of the larger single items, and our guide to wedding budgets and hidden costs shows where it fits.

The verdict

A lab-grown diamond engagement ring is a good choice if you want a large, well-cut diamond for less money and you are comfortable that it will not hold its value. It is a poorer choice if you want a store of value, or if the idea of a stone formed over millions of years matters to you. Neither answer is wrong. The mistake is paying a premium for a claim you did not check.

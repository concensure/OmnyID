Bachelorette favours: the party bag your friends quietly bin (and the ones they keep)

Topics in this guide

coupleswedding partyguestsStage: Details & invitesAustraliacreativityjoy

Every hens weekend has a moment when a party bag is handed out with a flourish. Inside are a tote with a name in glitter, a sachet of something pink, a temporary tattoo, three items of confectionery and a keyring. By Sunday afternoon, half of it has been left in the holiday house. That is the usual life of a bachelorette favour, and it does not have to be.

In Australia the same event is a hens party, a hens night or a hens weekend, and it is usually organised by the bridesmaids, with guests chipping in for costs. The favour is the small gift that sets the tone, and a well-chosen one helps people feel looked after, not marketed to. This guide covers what tends to get kept, what to leave out and a few things most organisers overlook, including the health and safety side of a bag full of party supplies.

Why the usual bag gets binned

  • It is full of novelties. Items that are funny for an evening, such as inflatable props, novelty straws and plastic sashes, have no life after the party.
  • Everything is branded. A bag covered in the bride's name or in-jokes is hard to reuse and makes guests feel like advertising.
  • It is not tailored. A bag designed around cocktails is unhelpful to guests who do not drink. A bag with a face mask is wasted on someone who has sensitive skin.
  • It is not packed for the day ahead. The best items answer a practical need at the right moment.

What guests actually keep

  • A good tote or bag. A sturdy canvas or cotton bag that does not look like a party prop gets reused for the market or the beach. Choose a simple design, and personalise with a small detail instead of a large slogan.
  • A stubby holder. An Australian favourite, cheap to personalise and genuinely used at barbecues for years. A good one is a cheeky reminder of the weekend.
  • Sun and skin care. A small sunscreen, lip balm with SPF and a hat on a summer weekend. Useful, and appreciated at an outdoor event.
  • Comfort items. Hair ties, blister plasters, a compact brush, a spare phone charger or portable battery. These solve problems people actually have.
  • A recovery kit. Water, electrolyte sachets, a snack bar and a small sachet of something salty. It can be the most appreciated item in the bag.
  • Something edible. A small box of chocolates, a packet of biscuits or a local treat. Check allergies. Our guide to favours guests actually keep explains why edible items usually disappear fastest.
  • A photograph. An instant photo or a small print from the weekend, with a handwritten note. Sentimental, and it takes no space.
  • A non-alcoholic drink. A well-made alcohol-free spirit mini or a sparkling drink in a small bottle suits guests who are not drinking, and everyone else too.

The medication question

Many hangover kits include painkillers. It sounds helpful, and it is a bad idea for the organiser to hand out medicines to a group. People have allergies, take other medications, are pregnant or have health conditions. Handing someone a pill they did not choose to take is risky for them and awkward for you. A safer route is to include electrolyte sachets, water and food, and let guests bring their own medicine. If someone asks for a painkiller, a chemist or pharmacist can advise.

Alcohol, safety and care

  • Plan for the people who do not drink. Pregnant guests, designated drivers, people in recovery and those who simply prefer not to should have drinks that look as good as everyone else's. Never make it a topic.
  • Do not hand out alcohol in a way that pushes it. A mini bottle in a bag is fine. A challenge, a drinking game or a forfeit that involves drinking is not.
  • Think about getting home. Arrange transport in advance, or share a list of safe options in the bag. A card with taxi and rideshare details and the address of the stay is useful.
  • Be wary of glitter. It is a microplastic, and it will be found in the car for months. Choose a biodegradable version or skip it.
  • Check any activities. Boats, tours and classes have their own age and safety rules, and some will not serve alcohol.

Money and fairness

Hens parties can quietly become the most expensive part of being a bridesmaid or guest. Avoid surprises. Agree a budget before you book, share the cost split in writing and be clear about what is optional. If you are the bride, check that the plans fit the group, because some friends will stay silent about cost rather than ask. Include favours in the budget: if you plan to give each guest a bag worth $25 to $30, that is a clear figure to add to the per-head total.

For a cost-effective option, put a single useful item in each bag rather than ten small ones. One good tote or stubby holder, plus a note, is cheaper and better than a pile of items that nobody will use.

Where to find them

  • Promotional product suppliers. Stubby holders, totes and caps with custom printing. Order at least three to four weeks ahead, and ask about minimum quantities.
  • Local makers and markets. Small-batch candles, soaps, snacks and prints, often with the option to personalise.
  • Discount stores and supermarkets. Good for blister plasters, sunscreens and snacks that you add yourself.
  • Party suppliers. Useful for table decorations, drink labels and themed items. Check what is single-use, and avoid more than you need.
  • Print shops. For photos, cards and a small itinerary printed for each guest.

Beyond the bag: ideas that cost less and mean more

  • A handwritten card per guest, saying why she is in the group of people the bride loves.
  • A printed schedule with times, addresses and a map, so nobody is asking about the plan all weekend.
  • A group activity as the gift, such as a class, a spa session or a picnic, paid for by the hosts.
  • A keepsake from the weekend, like a printed photo from each guest to the bride, or a shared message book.

A simple checklist

  1. Set a per-person budget and add it to the party costs.
  2. Ask about allergies, alcohol and any needs before you buy.
  3. Choose a few useful items, not a pile of novelties.
  4. Skip medicine, glitter and anything that pushes drinking.
  5. Order personalised items early, and pack the bags the night before.

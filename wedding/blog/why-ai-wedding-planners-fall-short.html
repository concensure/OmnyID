Why generic AI wedding tools frustrate so many couples

Topics in this guide

coupleswedding plannersStage: Plan & budgetdistrustconfidence

Ask any general-purpose AI chatbot for wedding help and you'll get a confident, well-written answer in seconds. That's exactly the problem. Confidence is easy to generate. A plan that actually holds up against your venue, your budget, and your family isn't. Over the past year, a fairly consistent pattern of complaints has emerged from couples who tried to plan a wedding through a generic AI assistant — and it's worth naming clearly, without pretending any one tool is uniquely at fault, because the pattern shows up across most of them.

It invents vendors that don't exist

This is the complaint that comes up most often, and it's the most consequential. General chatbots have no live connection to what's actually available in your area, so when they don't know something, they fill the gap with a plausible-sounding answer instead of saying so. That's shown up as florists that turn out not to exist, phone numbers that belong to someone else entirely, and pricing quoted with total confidence that turns out to be years out of date. At least one wedding planner has reported having to fire a photographer an AI tool had recommended, after the recommendation didn't hold up.

OmnyID treats this as a design constraint, not an edge case. Every vendor shown in your shortlist carries a source label and a confidence note, and anything that hasn't been verified says so plainly, with a clear next step to confirm it — instead of quietly presenting a guess as a fact.

It forgets everything the moment you close the tab

Open a generic chatbot and ask a wedding question, and you'll get a reasonable answer about weddings in general. Ask it about your wedding, and the problems start immediately — because it knows nothing about you. Your date, your venue, your guest count, who's RSVP'd, what's already booked: none of it carries over. Every session starts back at zero, and couples end up re-explaining their own wedding from scratch, again and again.

The most common complaint isn't that the advice is bad. It's that you have to re-teach the assistant your own wedding every single time you open it.

OmnyID's planning responses are backed by a persistent record, not a conversation that evaporates when the tab closes. Your wedding plan ID, your response workspace, the vendors you've already shortlisted, your budget assumptions and your guest count travel with you into every new conversation — so you finish a thought instead of restarting one.

It can talk about your budget, but it can't touch it

A chatbot can draft you a lovely seating strategy. What it typically can't do is see your actual guest list, update your actual budget, or add a florist it just found to a real comparison list sitting next to your other quotes. The advice and the actual planning materials live in two different places, and you become the manual bridge between them — copying numbers out of a chat window into a spreadsheet the assistant will never see again.

In OmnyID, the budget sheet, the guest list, and the vendor comparison table aren't described in a chat message — they're real files in your workspace, linked back to the response that created them, and updated in place as you make decisions.

Hidden fees still catch people off guard

Vendor management and unexpected costs come up again and again as one of the single biggest sources of wedding planning stress — delivery fees, vendor meals, overtime charges, all the line items nobody thinks to ask about up front. A chatbot that can't track "estimated" versus "confirmed" versus "paid" per vendor doesn't actually solve this problem; it just describes it more articulately.

OmnyID's budget artifacts carry all three figures side by side, plus a hidden-cost warning whenever a quote looks incomplete — so the gap between what you expected and what you're actually being charged shows up while there's still time to do something about it, not after the deposit has cleared.

It sounds confident even when it's guessing — and that can get risky

Confidence without grounding isn't just an inconvenience in a wedding context — it can be genuinely risky, because parts of a wedding are legally binding. In one widely reported case earlier this year, a couple's AI-generated ceremony script omitted wording their jurisdiction legally required, and their marriage wasn't valid as performed. That's an extreme outcome, but it's the sharp edge of a pattern: a tool that never says "I'm not sure, please confirm this with a professional" will eventually hand someone something that sounds right and isn't.

This is a hard line for OmnyID. It will track your NOIM timing and celebrant-related deadlines and remind you they're coming — but it will not claim to file legal paperwork, guarantee compliance, or write ceremony wording as if it were legal advice. Anything with real legal weight is flagged back to you, with a clear prompt to confirm it with your celebrant or the relevant authority.

Nothing actually gets done without you retyping it somewhere else

A generic assistant can write you a lovely email to a caterer. It generally can't send it, add the tasting to your calendar, or check it off a task list — so you copy the draft out, paste it into your real inbox, and manually update everything else it touched. The assistant produces text; you still do the coordination.

OmnyID keeps email drafts, calendar holds, and tasks inside the same approval-gated flow as everything else: you review a draft, approve it, and it's actually sent — with the deposit deadline it references already sitting on your calendar, not waiting for you to add it separately.

The pattern, not the product

None of this is really about any single tool being poorly built. It's what happens when a chat window with no memory, no live vendor data, and no connection to your actual files is asked to do a planning job that depends on all three. OmnyID isn't trying to be a cleverer version of that chat window. It's trying to be the private, remembering workspace underneath it — the part that was missing.

Casper Wu, OmnyID Weddings Written from patterns reported across couples' and planners' public reviews, not any single named product

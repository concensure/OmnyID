Can you get your wedding deposit back? What the contract and consumer law say

Topics in this guide

couplesvenuesvendorsStage: Plan & budgetAustraliaanxietysecurity

Nobody books a wedding venue expecting to cancel. But deposits are the biggest sum most couples pay before anything has happened, and the rules on getting them back are rarely explained until something goes wrong. This guide walks through the four situations couples actually face, what NSW Fair Trading and the ACCC say about each, and the questions to ask before you pay.

This is general information, not legal advice. Contract wording matters, state rules differ, and only a court or tribunal can decide whether a particular term is fair.

The short version

  • Paying a deposit can form a binding contract, so the terms you accepted apply.
  • If you simply change your mind, your refund usually depends on what the contract says.
  • If the supplier fails to deliver, or delivers badly, you have stronger rights under Australian Consumer Law.
  • Excessive cancellation charges can be challenged as unfair terms, but that is decided case by case.

Scenario 1: you change your mind or your plans change

NSW Fair Trading explains that paying a deposit or signing documents can mean you've entered a contract, and that pulling out may mean paying a penalty, sometimes up to the full contract amount. It also notes that the consumer cancellation rules don't give you a right to cancel a service and get a refund just because you changed your mind. In practice, your contract's cancellation clause decides what you get back.

So the real protection here is before you sign: read the clause, ask what is refundable and when, and get any promise in writing. See the checklist below.

Source: NSW Fair Trading, "Cancelling a service" and "Contracts and your consumer rights".

Scenario 2: the supplier fails to deliver

This is where couples have the strongest position. Under the Australian Consumer Law's consumer guarantees, services must be provided with due care and skill and within a reasonable time. If a supplier doesn't deliver at all, or delivers something seriously wrong, you can ask for a refund of advance payments and, in some cases, compensation for losses that were reasonably foreseeable. A "non-refundable" label doesn't remove these rights.

Examples: a photographer who accepts full payment and never delivers the gallery or album within a reasonable time, a caterer who doesn't turn up, a venue that closes before your date. Document everything: emails, receipts, dates.

Scenario 3: the cancellation fee feels excessive

The Australian Consumer Law doesn't ban deposits or cancellation fees, but it does allow unfair terms in standard consumer contracts to be challenged. A cancellation charge that is far larger than the supplier's genuine loss is the kind of term that can be questioned. We don't know of a published benchmark for wedding deposits. One Consumer Affairs Victoria industry guide on accommodation suggests a deposit shouldn't usually be more than 10% of the price unless the supplier's potential loss justifies more, but that guide is aimed at accommodation, so treat it as a reference point, not a rule. Fair Trading is clear that whether a term is unfair is for a court to decide.

If you think a term is unfair, start by raising it with the supplier in writing, quoting the clause, and ask for a reasonable alternative such as a credit or reschedule.

Source: NSW Fair Trading, "Unfair contract terms"; Consumer Affairs Victoria, "Travel and accommodation: an industry guide to the Australian Consumer Law".

Scenario 4: something outside anyone's control

During COVID restrictions, the ACCC's public line was that whether you can get a deposit back depends on the terms and conditions of each booking, so you should approach each vendor first and ask for a refund, a credit note or a postponement. It also said businesses can't change the terms afterwards to deny a refund you were entitled to when you booked. That advice was written for the pandemic, but the practical lesson holds for any event beyond your control (severe weather, a venue closure, an illness): your contract and the supplier's honesty decide the outcome, so ask early and in writing.

Ten things to check before you pay a deposit

  1. How much is the deposit as a share of the total, and when is the rest due?
  2. Is any part refundable, and until what date?
  3. What happens if the supplier cancels or closes? Do you get everything back?
  4. Can you move the date, and what does it cost?
  5. Is a credit note offered instead of a refund, and does it expire?
  6. What counts as "force majeure" in the contract, and who decides?
  7. What are the cancellation fees at 12 months, 6 months and 1 month out?
  8. Who is the legal entity you're paying (the business name, not only the brand)?
  9. Is everything agreed in the contract, not only in chats and calls?
  10. Do you have a receipt and a copy of the signed contract saved somewhere you can find in a year?

If it goes wrong: who to contact

  1. Write to the supplier. Be specific about what you want (refund, credit, redelivery) and by when.
  2. If they don't respond, contact your state or territory consumer protection agency. In NSW that's Fair Trading, which can help resolve disputes.
  3. For larger problems, consider independent legal advice. A community legal centre can often point you in the right direction.

Your turn: audit your contracts

  • Open every signed contract and find the cancellation clause.
  • Write each supplier's last refund date next to it in your plan.
  • Flag any you don't understand and ask the supplier to explain it in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get my wedding deposit back if I change my mind?

Usually it depends on the contract. NSW Fair Trading says paying a deposit can form a contract, and the consumer cancellation rules don't give a right to a refund just for changing your mind. Read the cancellation clause before you pay.

What if my wedding supplier doesn't deliver?

Under the Australian Consumer Law, services must be provided with due care and skill and within a reasonable time. If a supplier fails, you can seek a refund of advance payments and in some cases compensation. A non-refundable label does not remove these rights.

Can a wedding cancellation fee be unfair?

Excessive cancellation charges can be challenged under the unfair contract terms rules, but only a court can decide whether a term is unfair. Start by raising it with the supplier in writing.

Who do I contact if I can't resolve a wedding dispute?

Contact your state or territory consumer protection agency, such as NSW Fair Trading. For larger amounts, consider independent legal advice or a community legal centre.

How little do you legally need to get married in Australia? Less than you think

Topics in this guide

couplescelebrantsStage: Celebrant & suppliersAustraliauncertaintyconfidence

Strip away the venue, the dress, the 120 guests and the playlist, and a legal marriage in Australia comes down to a short list of things. That's useful to know, whether you're planning a large wedding and want to see what's tradition versus requirement, or you're thinking small, a micro-wedding or an elopement. Here is the list, sorted into what the law requires, what your venue or celebrant may require, and what is simply your choice.

This guide covers the essentials for a civil marriage under the Marriage Act 1961. Religious ceremonies and special circumstances can differ, so confirm the details with your celebrant and the Attorney-General's Department.

The legal minimum, in one box

  • An authorised celebrant, who is physically present.
  • A Notice of Intended Marriage lodged with the celebrant no earlier than 18 months and no later than one month before the wedding.
  • Evidence of each person's date and place of birth.
  • Two witnesses aged over 18, present at the ceremony.
  • The celebrant's legally required words (the monitum), and your vows.
  • Three marriage certificates signed by both of you, your celebrant and both witnesses.

Source: Attorney-General's Department, "Get married", and "Notice of Intended Marriage". Details can change, so check the current pages.

Part 1: what the law requires

An authorised celebrant

Civil celebrants must be authorised by the Commonwealth. Before you book, check your celebrant is registered. It's the one vendor decision with a legal consequence.

Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM)

You give it to your celebrant, no earlier than 18 months and no later than a month before the date. The one-month deadline is the hard one. Our legal checklist covers what to do if time is short.

Two witnesses and the legal words

Two witnesses aged over 18 must be present. For a civil ceremony the celebrant says the prescribed statement (the monitum) and you each say your vows to the legal wording. Your celebrant will give you the current text. After the ceremony, three certificates are signed, and one goes to the state or territory registry.

Part 2: what a venue, council or vendor may require

These come from contracts and permits, not the Marriage Act, so they vary:

  • Permits for parks, beaches and some public land, plus rules on candles, confetti and fixing decorations. See our DIY decor guide.
  • Minimum spends, finishing times and guest caps (curfews and package hours at NSW venues).
  • Deposits, cancellation terms and insurance (your rights on deposits).

Part 3: what is entirely your choice

None of the following is a legal requirement. Plenty of couples love them, and plenty skip them:

The legal moment can be short. The rest is the story you choose to build around it.

One detail that trips people up: the certificate

The decorative certificate you sign for a keepsake isn't your proof of marriage. For name changes and most official purposes you need an official certificate from the registry in the state or territory where you married. NSW Government says ceremonial or commemorative certificates are not accepted. We cover what to do next in changing your name after marriage.

Planning small? Three honest notes

  1. Small doesn't mean cheap by default. Per-head prices and minimum spends can make a 20-person wedding cost more per guest than a 100-person one.
  2. Public spaces need permission. A beach or park ceremony may still need a council permit, even for a handful of people.
  3. The paperwork is identical. A two-witness ceremony needs the same NOIM and certificates as a large one.

Your turn: the "keep or skip" exercise

  • Each of you, separately, list the five parts of a wedding you'd miss most if they were gone.
  • Then list the five you'd be happy to drop.
  • Compare. Where you agree on what to drop is your first budget saving.

What is the one "must-have" you realised isn't actually required? Many couples find that the legal part is the easy bit and the expectations are the heavy bit.

Frequently asked questions

What do you legally need to get married in Australia?

An authorised celebrant, a Notice of Intended Marriage lodged between 18 months and one month before the wedding, evidence of your date and place of birth, two witnesses over 18, the legally required words and vows, and signed marriage certificates.

Do I need a venue or guests to get married in Australia?

No. The law requires the celebrant, the two of you and two witnesses. A venue, guests, rings, a dress and a reception are your choice, although public places may need a permit.

How many witnesses do I need to get married in Australia?

Two witnesses aged over 18 must be present at the ceremony and sign the marriage certificates.

Is the decorative marriage certificate legally valid?

No. For name changes and most official purposes you need an official certificate from the registry. NSW Government says ceremonial or commemorative certificates are not accepted.

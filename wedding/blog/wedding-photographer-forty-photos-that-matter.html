Your wedding photographer will take thousands of pictures. You will love about forty.

Topics in this guide

couplesphotographersStage: Celebrant & suppliersnostalgiaclarity

Hire a wedding photographer and you are not really buying a day of coverage. You are buying a handful of photographs: the one of your parents standing together, the one of your grandmother laughing, the portrait where you both look like yourselves. Over decades, a gallery of hundreds of images shrinks, in practice, to a few dozen that get framed, printed, shared and cried over.

The good news is that those photographs are easier to plan for than they are to leave to chance. This guide is about what to prioritise once you already have a photographer, or are choosing between two. If you are still shortlisting, start with how to choose a photographer you will still love in 20 years and the contract and coverage questions.

The forty that matter

There is no magic number, and nobody counts. But if you imagine the images you would choose for an album, a living-room wall and a parent's phone, they tend to fall into a few groups.

The point of the exercise is to spot what is missing from your plan. Most couples have a timeline for the couple portraits and the group shot, and nothing for the grandmother, the best friend who flew in or the five minutes when everyone is relaxed.

Light decides more than lenses

A good photographer can handle most conditions, but timing still shapes the result. Midday sun creates hard shadows and squinting. Soft, low light in the hour before sunset is flattering and forgiving. A few practical consequences:

  • Check the sunset time for your date. It is easy to find, and it tells you when the best portrait light will happen. Ask your photographer to build the timeline backwards from it.
  • Plan couple portraits for the best light, not the most convenient slot. Ten minutes at sunset often beats forty minutes at 2pm. Schedule it in, and tell your venue and caterer, because it will pull you away from the party.
  • Think about shade and rain. Where does the photographer take portraits if it pours? Ask them to scout the venue and find indoor spots with good window light.
  • Time of day and ceremony. A ceremony in harsh sun means guests squint and the couple does too. Consider a later start, or a shaded location.

The "first look"

A first look is a private moment when you see each other before the ceremony, with the photographer present. It has become common. The benefits are less pressure, more time for portraits in better light and a more relaxed cocktail hour, since the formal photographs are done. The downside is that some couples love the surprise of the aisle, and you give that up. It is not better or worse, only a trade-off to decide on deliberately.

Family formals without the herding

The family group photographs are the part of the day most likely to run long and go wrong, and the one most likely to matter later.

  • Write the list in advance. A short list of six to eight groupings is usually enough. Name the people in each group, in order.
  • Appoint a wrangler. A confident friend or relative who knows everyone and has the list in hand. The photographer's job is photographing, not hunting for an uncle.
  • Plan for complicated families. Divorced parents, step-families and estranged relatives need thinking through, kindly and in advance. Ask whom you want in which groups, and tell your photographer privately.
  • Take the individual ones too. A portrait of each grandparent alone, taken in two minutes, is the photograph people ask for years later.

A shot list, or a moments list

Handing a photographer a list of 100 poses can backfire, because it turns them into a technician and you into a prop. A better approach is a short "moments list" with only what is specific to you:

  • People who must be photographed, and in what combinations.
  • Objects with a story: a grandmother's brooch, a handwritten letter, a family dress.
  • Cultural or religious moments the photographer may not know in advance, with times.
  • Surprises: a flash mob, a guest arriving late, a speech by someone special.
  • Things you do not want: a particular pose, photographs of a certain relative, close-ups of something you are self-conscious about.

Then leave the rest to their eye. That is what you hired.

Styles and trends, in plain language

  • Documentary or candid. Photographers who stay in the background and capture moments as they unfold. It suits couples who dislike posing, and it depends on your photographer being quick and attentive.
  • Editorial and posed. More directed, with composed portraits. Ideal if you want striking images and are comfortable being directed.
  • Film. A revival of film photography, with its soft colour and grain. It can be beautiful, and it is slower, costs more and gives you less immediate feedback.
  • On-camera flash at receptions. A direct-flash look has become fashionable for party photographs. Ask to see a full reception gallery from a single wedding, not only highlights, so you know what you are getting.

Questions that are newer than the old ones

  • How do you edit and cull? Many photographers now use software, including AI-assisted tools, to sort and process thousands of frames. Ask what is automated, and whether any of your images are uploaded to third-party services.
  • May you use my photographs in marketing? A typical contract gives the photographer the right to show your images. If you want privacy, particularly for children, put it in writing.
  • How long until I see a preview, and the full gallery? Many couples receive a handful of images within days and the full set within weeks or months. Ask for a date in the contract.
  • How are my files stored? Backups, cloud storage and how long the gallery will stay available. Download everything yourself and keep it somewhere safe.

What helps, and what hurts, on the day

  • An unplugged ceremony. A polite sign or an announcement asking guests to put phones away stops a sea of screens from appearing in your aisle photographs. If you would rather not, a short note on the wedding website works too.
  • A clear timeline. Share it with your photographer, celebrant, venue and MC. A photographer who finds out the ceremony has moved by thirty minutes is working against the clock.
  • A meal for the photographer. Coverage often runs for many hours, and a photographer who is fed and watered shoots better. Many contracts ask for a vendor meal. Put it in the catering numbers.
  • The right amount of coverage. If the reception runs long, a shorter package may miss the last dance. Our guide to fake exits explains one way couples manage photographer hours and still get the send-off shot.
  • An engagement session. A short shoot a few months before is a rehearsal for being photographed. Most couples are tense in front of a camera at first, and the wedding is not the best place to find out.

Where to find a wedding photographer

  • Venue and celebrant recommendations. Photographers who have worked at your venue know the light and the layout.
  • Professional associations. Bodies such as the Australian Institute of Professional Photography list members and set standards. Membership is not a guarantee of fit, but it is a place to start.
  • Directories and social media. Useful for browsing styles. Always ask for a full gallery from a recent wedding to see how they handle a whole day, not only the highlights.
  • Friends and recently married couples. A recommendation from someone who still loves their photographs is the best signal there is.

The only checklist that matters

  1. Check sunset and build your portrait window around it.
  2. Write your family list and appoint a wrangler.
  3. Make a short moments list, not a hundred poses.
  4. Put delivery dates, usage and backups in the contract.
  5. Feed your photographer, and tell them about every change to the timeline.

Do those five things and the forty photographs that matter will be taken, whether or not you ever count them.

Married at the movies: the surprisingly hard parts of a cinema wedding

Topics in this guide

A movie theatre wedding has an obvious pitch. There is a stage that is already lit, a few hundred comfortable seats facing the front, a sound system that makes a whispered vow audible in the back row, and a screen the size of a house. For a couple who met over a shared obsession with a director, a franchise or one very specific 1980s comedy, it can feel like the only venue that makes sense.

It is also a venue that was never designed for a wedding, which is exactly where the surprises hide. A cinema is a commercial business with a film schedule, a liquor licence, a cleaning contract and a landlord, and none of those are flexible in the way a wedding venue's usually are. This guide covers why couples choose cinemas, where to find ones that say yes, and the questions that decide whether it will be magic or a long afternoon in a dark room.

Why a cinema wedding keeps turning up on wishlists

Unusual venues have been climbing the wishlist for some time, as couples look for places that feel like them rather than like the fourth ballroom of the weekend. A cinema suits that mood for several practical reasons as well as sentimental ones.

  • It is weatherproof. No marquee, no plan B for rain, no wind in the veil.
  • The seating exists. Guests can sit comfortably for a long ceremony, and everyone has a view. Nobody is standing at the back on a lawn.
  • The audio-visual kit is already there. Photo slideshows, a film made by your friends or a recorded message from a relative overseas can play on a screen that does it properly.
  • It has a built-in theme. Tickets as invitations, a programme designed as a poster, seating by row and seat number: the ideas almost write themselves.
  • It is memorable. Guests who have been to a dozen weddings will remember the one at the pictures.

Where to look

There is no single directory of wedding-friendly cinemas, so you search the way you would for any unusual venue: by asking. Start with these.

  • Independent and art-house cinemas. These are the most likely to welcome a ceremony, and some already list private hire for parties and corporate events. A bar and a foyer often come with the auditorium.
  • Heritage picture palaces. Older single-screen cinemas have the architecture couples love, with curved balconies, original ceilings and proper curtains. They also tend to have stairs, narrow doorways and limited accessibility, so check that carefully.
  • Chain cinemas. Many large operators have a private-hire or events team. They are strong on equipment and process and weaker on atmosphere, and they may not allow a ceremony or decorations.
  • Community, university and museum theatres. Some council and arts-centre cinemas hire out at lower rates and are used to hosting events.
  • Outdoor and rooftop screens. Seasonal cinemas in gardens and on rooftops often cater for private events, with the trade-off that they depend on the weather and on council and noise rules.

Search for "private hire", "venue hire" or "events" on a cinema's website, then phone. A direct call about a wedding usually gets a clearer answer than a general enquiry form, because it reaches someone who can say whether it has been done before.

Two Australian cinemas to start with

These are starting points rather than endorsements. Both official venue pages describe event hire, but published wedding details are incomplete, so confirm the current capacity, access, licence conditions, pricing and availability before relying on them.

  • James Theatre Dungog, Hunter Region NSW. A community-run heritage cinema with a stage, dance floor, food-preparation space and outdoor garden. Check the official hire information.
  • The Roxy Theatre Bingara, North West NSW. A restored 1936 Art Deco cinema that the council describes as a function centre for weddings and other events. Check the official venue information.

The legal part is the easy part

In Australia a marriage can take place almost anywhere, as long as you have given your celebrant a valid Notice of Intended Marriage, two adult witnesses are present, and the celebrant is registered. The ceremony does not need an approved building. So the question is never "is it legal in a cinema?" but "will this cinema let us?". That answer sits in the venue's contract, not in the Marriage Act.

The questions that decide it

Ask the cinema these before you pay a deposit
QuestionWhy it matters
Is a wedding ceremony allowed in the auditorium?Some venues only allow screenings or parties. A ceremony may be refused outright.
Can we bring our own film or footage?Your own video is usually simple. A commercial film is a different matter, covered below.
What is the liquor licence, and can we bring our own drinks?Cinemas with a licence usually have to sell the alcohol served, which affects your drinks budget.
Can outside caterers or a cake come in?Many cinemas run their own food service and limit outside food.
How long do we get, including set-up and pack-down?Cinemas run films on a schedule. The auditorium may only be free for a short window.
What can we put on walls and seats?Tape, confetti, petals and candles are often banned. Real flames almost always are.
How many people can the room hold, and is access step-free?Capacity is fixed by seat count and fire rules. Raked floors are hard for wheelchairs and walking frames.
Who runs the projector and sound on the day?A technician is often an extra cost, and a mistimed slideshow is easy to avoid with a rehearsal.

Screening a commercial film

This is the one most couples miss. A cinema holds the right to show films it has booked through a distributor, and a private screening of a feature is generally arranged through that same distributor, often with a fee. You cannot simply plug in a streaming service or a disc and play it to your guests in a public venue, because those licences are for private home viewing. Ask the cinema whether a specific film is available for a private session, what it costs, and how far ahead they need to know. A film that is currently in release, or one from a studio that is difficult to reach, may not be possible at all.

A safer, and often more personal, option is to make your own short film. A three-minute montage of how you met, with friends dropping in cameos, plays beautifully on a cinema screen and has no licensing complications beyond the music you choose, which is worth checking with the venue.

What goes wrong, and how to head it off

  • The aisle is raked. Most auditoriums slope toward the screen, and many have steps beside the seats. High heels, a long train and low lighting are a poor combination. Walk it with your shoes on before the day.
  • The room is too dark for photos. Cinemas are designed to be dim. Ask whether house lights can stay up for the ceremony, and brief your photographer so they can plan lighting. A good photographer will scout it first; see how to brief for the photos that matter.
  • The reception has nowhere to go. Fixed seating means no dancing in the auditorium. Plan the party for the foyer, the bar or a second venue nearby, and do not assume the foyer can hold everyone standing.
  • The sound is brilliant, for films. A cinema's speakers are tuned for a soundtrack. A live acoustic guitarist or a string quartet in a room built to absorb sound can feel flat, so ask for a sound check.
  • Food and drink rules shape the menu. A cinema that only serves its own menu may limit you to popcorn, confectionery and a bar. That can be fun for a casual cocktail-hour celebration and a problem for a three-course sit-down dinner.
  • Hard cut-off times. Staff, cleaners and the next session are waiting. Agree the finish time in writing and add a buffer.

Three ways to do it

  1. Ceremony in the cinema, party elsewhere. Hold the ceremony and a short screening in the auditorium, then move to a restaurant, bar or garden for the meal and dancing. This uses the cinema for what it does best and sidesteps its limits.
  2. The micro-wedding. A small guest list, a celebrant, a private screening of something you love, and dinner. Our guide to a small ceremony with a bigger reception covers how to split the day.
  3. The screening as the entertainment. Marry somewhere conventional and take guests to the cinema afterwards for a private film or your own short film. It is cheaper, it removes the ceremony restrictions, and it still delivers the theme.

Ideas that suit the setting

  • Design the invitation and order of service as a ticket and a poster, with guests' seats assigned by row and number.
  • Show a short film before the ceremony: a story of how you met, narrated by your closest friends.
  • Use the foyer for the first drinks, and run a candy bar or a popcorn station that doubles as a favour.
  • Hold the speeches on the stage with the screen behind, and have a friend or videographer play a live feed of the speaker for the back rows.
  • Play the credits at the end: names of everyone who helped, with a final message from you.

Is it worth it?

A cinema wedding is worth it if the venue itself is part of your story, you are comfortable with a smaller guest list and a tighter timetable, and you are prepared to do a site visit and a rehearsal rather than turn up on the day and hope. It is probably not worth it if you want a long, free-flowing night with your own caterer, a dance floor in the main room and fewer rules. The honest answer is a hybrid: use the cinema for the moment it does best, and borrow a more flexible venue for everything else.

If you are weighing it against other offbeat options, read the hidden costs of unconventional venues, and use our venue tour questions when you visit. Whatever you decide, get every promise about hours, decorations, alcohol and screening in writing.

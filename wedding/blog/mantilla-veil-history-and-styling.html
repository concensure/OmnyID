Why the mantilla veil is the most misunderstood thing on a bride's head

Topics in this guide

couplesdressmakersstylistsStage: Details & invitescuriosityconfidence

Put a mantilla veil on a bride and it changes the whole picture. There is no puff of tulle at the crown, no blusher over the face and no train of net behind. Instead a single piece of lace rises from a comb at the back of the head, frames the face and falls softly over the shoulders. It looks old and new at once, which is exactly why it has become one of the most requested bridal accessories.

It is also one of the least understood. The mantilla is not just "a lace veil with a scalloped edge". It comes from a specific Spanish tradition, with religious meaning, regional variations and some rules that most brides never hear about. This guide explains where it comes from, how it differs from the veils in a bridal shop, how to wear one well and what to ask before you buy. Where historical details rest on bridal and reference sources of varying quality, we say so.

What a mantilla actually is

A mantilla is a lace or silk veil worn over the head and shoulders, traditionally held in place with a tall decorative comb called a peineta. Historical accounts trace it to Spain, and several sources date its development to the 16th century. The word comes from the Spanish for a small cloak or cape. In the centuries that followed, women wore it to Mass and on other formal occasions, and it carried Christian associations of modesty and head covering.

It is not the same thing as a standard bridal veil. A bridal veil is usually tulle, often in layers, and is worn to cover or frame the face or to trail behind the bride. A mantilla is usually a single layer of lace, with a decorated border all the way round, and it is worn to drape over the head and shoulders.

A lace story

As lace became more elaborate and valuable through the 17th and 18th centuries, so did the mantilla. Sources describe Spanish lacemakers working in several traditions, including styles associated with the French towns of Chantilly and Alençon, and a silk lace known as blonde that was made largely for mantillas. Chantilly lace is generally described as light and airy, while Alençon has raised cord outlines that give it a bolder, more defined pattern. One reference notes that much of the lace-making was done by women working outside the male-dominated guilds. We could not verify every detail in an academic source, so treat the specifics as the trade's account rather than settled history.

Black, white and the "privilege of white"

The colour has meaning. In Spain, black mantillas are the traditional choice for religious processions such as Holy Week, and for formal occasions. At Spanish weddings, the mother of the groom or the godmother traditionally wears a black mantilla and a peineta, while the bride wears white or ivory. Sources also describe a custom known as the privilège du blanc, under which a white mantilla was reserved for a few Catholic queens and consorts when meeting the Pope. That does not stop a modern bride choosing white or ivory, but it is a good reminder that the garment has a code behind it.

Is it okay if you are not Catholic or Spanish?

Yes. People of many faiths and none wear mantillas, and bridal designers sell them widely. A few points will help you wear one respectfully.

  • Know the origin. The mantilla is closely tied to Spanish and Latin American Catholic tradition, and also appears in Filipino and other communities. If it is part of your own heritage, you may want to mention it in your ceremony or programme.
  • Check the venue. In a church, ask what is customary. Head coverings for women are no longer a general requirement in the Catholic Church, but some choose to wear one, and a mantilla will not look out of place.
  • Name it correctly. A "mantilla-style" veil, made in tulle with a lace edge, is a different thing from a lace mantilla. Sellers sometimes use the term loosely.
  • Avoid costume. A black mantilla with a flamenco look can read as fancy dress if the rest of the outfit is not designed around it. A softer ivory or white mantilla in a modern shape is easier to wear.

How to style a mantilla

  • Hair. A high bun, a low chignon or a sleek half-up style gives the comb something to hold. Very loose hair can work, but needs extra pins to stop the comb sliding.
  • Neckline. A simple neckline, such as a V, a bateau or a high neck, lets the lace be the feature. A heavily embroidered bodice and a heavily patterned mantilla compete.
  • Jewellery. Keep it minimal. Large earrings and a necklace can crowd the lace and snag the edge.
  • Length. A short mantilla frames the face, while a longer one sweeps behind like a train. Choose according to the dress and venue.
  • The comb. A tall peineta is traditional and dramatic. A smaller comb gives a more modern look. Wear whichever you can keep in place for hours.
  • With the face. Some brides drape the lace over the face for the aisle and lift it at the altar. The lace should be fine enough to see through, and you should practise the lift.

What to look out for when you buy

  • Hand-made or machine-made. Genuine hand-made mantillas, particularly in fine silk lace, are expensive and may be heirloom pieces. Machine-made versions are more affordable and can be very beautiful. Ask which you are looking at, and be wary of "heirloom" claims on very cheap pieces.
  • The border. The scalloped edge is the signature. Check it is even and that the lace does not fray at the edge.
  • The weight and the comb. A heavy mantilla on a flimsy comb will slide. Ask how it is attached, and test it with movement.
  • Size. Measure from the crown to where you want it to fall, and think about how it will look from behind and from the side.
  • Return policy. If you order from overseas, ask about customs, shipping time and returns.
  • Where to look. Bridal boutiques that stock mantillas, independent designers, Spanish and Latin American specialty shops, vintage dealers and made-to-order makers. Some families have a mantilla to pass down, which can be the most meaningful choice of all.

Wearing it on the day

  • Practise with the hairstyle. Do a trial with the comb and mantilla together, then move your head, hug someone and sit down.
  • Bring a helper. Someone who knows how the lace should fall, and who can fix it before photographs.
  • Think about weather. Lace this light catches the wind. For an outdoor ceremony, choose a slightly heavier lace or a shorter length, and brief your photographer.
  • Store it flat afterwards. Keep it in acid-free tissue in a cool, dry place so it can be handed on.

Why it is trending

Brides are leaning toward simpler, more personal attire, and the mantilla suits that mood. It is less fussy than a cathedral veil and more distinctive than a plain tulle one, and it photographs well from every angle. If you are exploring attire that is a little unconventional, see our guide to buying a wedding dress online and wearing what you like, and our overview of Australian wedding trends.

Choose one because you love how it looks and respect where it comes from, and it will do what it has done for centuries: frame a face, and make the people watching go quiet for a moment.

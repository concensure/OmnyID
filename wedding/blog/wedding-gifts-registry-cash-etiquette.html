The wedding gift nobody wants to talk about: who gives what, who is keeping score

Topics in this guide

Wedding gifts sit in an awkward spot in the planning process. Everyone has a view about what is polite, hardly anyone says it out loud, and couples are left guessing whether to ask for money, a toaster or nothing at all. Guests worry about the opposite problem: how much is enough, and what will be noticed.

There is no single Australian rule, and the "cover your plate" idea, which says a gift should at least equal what the couple spent on you, is a rumour more than a law. What follows is a practical guide to the main options, how to ask without awkwardness and how to say thank you in a way that people remember.

What couples can ask for

Couples have more choices than they once did, and each suits a different situation.

  • A traditional gift registry. A list at a department store or online retailer of homewares and appliances. It suits couples setting up a new home, and gives guests a clear, concrete option. The drawback is that many couples already have a full kitchen, and guests who live far away may find shipping awkward.
  • A wishing well or cash. In Australia a wishing well, a decorated box or card holder where guests leave a card with money, is common. It suits couples who would simply rather have money, and who want a flexible option for guests at the reception.
  • A honeymoon or experience fund. Guests contribute toward flights, a stay or a special activity, often through an online fund. It feels more personal than plain cash, though not everyone likes giving to a trip.
  • A home deposit or savings fund. Increasingly common among couples facing high housing costs. It is honest, and some guests will like the clarity.
  • A donation to a cause. Some couples ask for gifts to a charity instead of presents. Be specific about the charity and how to give.
  • No gifts. "Your presence is the only gift we need" is a valid choice. Mean it, or guests will bring something anyway, and you may be left with gifts you did not want.

How to ask without sounding greedy

Etiquette sources generally agree on one point: gift requests do not belong on the invitation itself. They belong on the wedding website, in a note passed on by family or in answer to a guest who asks. Our guide to wedding websites and invitation wording has examples of gentle phrasing.

  • Be warm and brief. "Your company means the world to us. If you would like to give a gift, here are some ideas" is enough.
  • Put the options in one place. A short list on the website beats a message from a relative that gets garbled.
  • Offer a range. Include something at several price points, or note that no gift is expected. Nobody should feel their attendance is not enough.
  • Be honest about what you want. A couple who already own a toaster should not register for one. Guests can tell, and appreciate directness.

Cultural traditions to respect

Many cultures have their own gift customs, and Australian weddings often mix them. Red envelopes with money are traditional at Chinese weddings. South Asian weddings may include cash gifts called shagun. Other communities have their own traditions around money dances, gold or family gifts. If your wedding combines traditions, explain on your website what guests can expect, so nobody feels wrong-footed.

What guests give, and why the "right" amount does not exist

If you are a guest, there is no formula that works for everyone. A sensible approach is to think about how close you are to the couple, what you can comfortably afford and what attending cost you. If you flew interstate, paid for accommodation and bought an outfit, you have already given a lot. Cash is usually welcome if there is no registry, a gift from the registry is safe, and something personal can be perfect if you know the couple well. A heartfelt card often matters more than the amount in it.

Handling gifts on the day

  • Provide a secure card box. A lockable or secure box, watched by someone you trust, is better than an open basket. Cash and cards are easy to lose in a crowded room.
  • Ask guests to send large gifts to a home address. Nobody wants to carry a boxed blender through a reception, and it is the sort of thing that gets forgotten. Put the address on the website.
  • Appoint a gift minder. A friend or relative who takes charge of cards and parcels at the end of the night and delivers them somewhere safe.
  • Take care with online funds. If you are collecting money by bank transfer or through a platform, double-check the link on your website, and warn guests that scammers sometimes copy payment details. Ask guests to use the link on your own site, not one forwarded in a message from someone else.

A log that saves friendships

Keep a list as gifts arrive: who gave what, the date and whether you have thanked them. It sounds clinical, and it is the only way to be sure that you have not missed anyone. A shared spreadsheet or a list in your planning tool works, and your future self will be grateful when you are writing cards weeks later.

Thank-you notes: the part everyone remembers

  • Write them. A personal note is still the standard. Email or a message is acceptable for some people and some circumstances, but handwritten tends to be appreciated.
  • Name the gift, and say what it means. "Thank you for the casserole dish, we cooked our first dinner in it" is far better than "thanks for the gift". For cash, say what you plan to use it for.
  • Give yourself a timeline. Etiquette advice usually suggests sending notes within a few weeks of the wedding, or of receiving a gift if it arrives later. Late is better than never.
  • Split the load. Each of you writes to your own side of the guest list, and you share the rest. It will take far less time than you expect.
  • Thank the helpers. Parents, wedding party and friends who gave time, not a present, deserve thanks too. A small gift of your own is a good way to do it. See bridesmaid gifts and food and drink gifts for ideas.

Gifts from the couple

Gifting is also about what you give. It is traditional to thank parents, the wedding party and anyone who contributed significantly, and many couples exchange a small gift with each other on the wedding morning. A gift does not need to be expensive. It needs to show that you noticed what they did. Plan the list early, set a budget and add it to your overall wedding budget. Our guide to wedding budgets, tipping and hidden costs is a good place to see where gifts typically fit.

A short decision guide

  1. Decide what you actually need or want, and be honest about it.
  2. Choose one main option and one backup, such as a registry plus a wishing well.
  3. Put it on your website, in a single short paragraph.
  4. Plan how gifts and cards are collected and stored on the day.
  5. Keep a log, write thank-you notes and say thanks to the people who helped.

Done well, gifting is a gentle way to let people who love you take part in the start of your married life, and nobody has to do the arithmetic.

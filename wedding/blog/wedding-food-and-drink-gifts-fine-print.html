Edible, drinkable and a bit risky: the fine print of wedding food and drink gifts

Topics in this guide

couplesguestsvendorsStage: Details & invitesAustraliacuriosityconfidence

Food and drink make some of the best wedding gifts. A bottle of local wine for the parents, a jar of honey on each guest's place setting, a hamper for the friends who flew in, a box of good chocolate for the vendors who stayed late: these are easy to love because they get used. They also carry risks that a candle or a keyring never will. They melt, spoil, leak, trigger allergies and, in some cases, cannot legally be sent to certain people at all.

This guide covers the trend, where to find good options, and the checks that stop a thoughtful gift from turning into a problem. It is about practical gifting for the people around your wedding. For the wider question of what guests give the couple, see our guide to registries, cash and etiquette.

Why edible and drinkable gifts are everywhere

Couples increasingly say they would rather give something consumed than something stored. The reasoning is simple: nobody has to find shelf space for a jar of jam. There is also a growing interest in local makers, regional produce and non-alcoholic craft drinks, and Australian native flavours such as lemon myrtle, wattleseed and Davidson plum have turned up in everything from tea blends to chocolate. Small producers often welcome wedding orders, which makes it a good way to support a local business and give something with a story. The fine print

Alcohol

  • Age. Alcohol can only be supplied to adults. Delivery services in Australia generally require someone aged 18 or over to be present and often to show ID or sign, so a gift sent to an empty house may be returned.
  • Delivery rules. Rules differ between states and territories, and retailers manage the checks. If you are sending to another state, ask the retailer whether it can deliver and how long it will take.
  • Do not force it on people. Some guests do not drink for health, faith or personal reasons. A thoughtful alternative, such as premium non-alcoholic drinks or sparkling juice, avoids awkwardness.
  • Gifting at the venue. A venue with a liquor licence may not allow you to bring and give out bottles on site. Ask before you buy.

Allergies and dietary needs

  • Know the common allergens. Nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, sesame, fish and shellfish are the usual culprits. If you do not know a recipient's allergies, ask or choose something simple.
  • Label what you make. Packaged food sold in shops must carry allergen information under Australian food standards. A homemade gift is a different matter, but you should still list the main ingredients and allergens on a tag.
  • Offer a choice. Vegan, gluten-free, halal and kosher guests are common. One flexible option, such as a jar of honey or a pack of tea, is easier than catering to each need separately.

Homemade gifts and food safety

Homemade jam, chutney or biscuits are lovely and personal. If you make them, follow a tested recipe, use clean, sterilised jars, add a date and storage instructions and avoid home preserving methods that are known to be risky, such as canning low-acid foods in oil. Ask a trusted source or your local council if you are unsure. When in doubt, buy from a professional producer.

Heat, shipping and travel

  • Australian summers are hard on food. Chocolate melts, cheese sweats and cakes dry out. If your wedding is in warm weather, choose items that tolerate heat and never leave a gift hamper in a car.
  • Check lead times. Small makers can take weeks to fulfil a wedding order. Hamper companies are often booked out around Christmas and Mother's Day.
  • Plan for breakage. Glass bottles and jars need padding, and a courier can be rough. Ask how the supplier packs.
  • Flying with gifts. Liquids in carry-on bags are limited, and checked luggage has weight limits. Check your airline's rules.

Moving food across borders

Australia has strict biosecurity rules. Bringing food into the country from overseas is tightly controlled, and items such as honey, dairy, meat, fruit and plant products are often restricted. You must declare all food on arrival, and penalties for not doing so can be serious. If you are travelling or posting gifts from overseas, check the Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry's guidance before you buy. Within Australia, some states and regions limit the movement of fresh produce, plants and certain bee products, so check before you send a regional gift interstate.

Where to find good options

  • Local producers. Farmers' markets, makers' markets, cellar doors, craft breweries, distilleries and regional food trails. Ask about wedding or bulk pricing, and whether they will add a custom label.
  • Specialty grocers and delis. They often assemble hampers to a budget and will deliver or wrap.
  • Bottle shops and online retailers. Many will gift-wrap, add a card and handle age verification and delivery.
  • Hamper companies. Convenient and well-packed, though less personal. Check what is in the box and what it costs separately.
  • Bakers and chocolatiers. Good for boxes of biscuits, brownies or truffles. Order early, and ask about shelf life.

Budget and planning

  • Count recipients first. Parents, wedding party, out-of-town guests and vendors can add up quickly. Make a list with names and a spending limit for each group.
  • Add the invisible costs. Delivery, wrapping, tags and cards often add to the bill. Our wedding budget guide shows how small items grow.
  • Order two to three months ahead for custom items, and confirm quantities and delivery dates closer to the day.
  • Plan the handover. Who delivers the vendor thank-yous, who hands out the welcome bags and who takes the leftovers home?

For more ideas aimed at guests, see personalised wedding favour ideas.

A short checklist

  1. Decide who you are gifting and set a budget for each group.
  2. Ask about allergies, diets and alcohol, or choose something safe for most people.
  3. Check delivery rules, lead times and heat.
  4. Label ingredients and add storage instructions.
  5. Plan how and when each gift will be handed over.

A good edible gift is one that gets eaten, enjoyed and remembered with a smile. The fine print is what makes sure of it.

What does a marriage celebrant actually do?

Topics in this guide

couplescelebrantsStage: Celebrant & suppliersAustraliacuriositycalm

Ask most couples what a celebrant does and you'll hear "marries us". That's true, and it's about a third of the job. The rest is the part nobody sees on a booking page: turning your story into a ceremony, keeping a hundred nervous people calm, and making sure the legal bits happen without anyone noticing them.

This guide explains the three jobs a marriage celebrant does in Australia, what a good one feels like in the room, and how that differs from a registry office. If you're still looking for one, start with how to find a marriage celebrant in Australia.

The short answer

A marriage celebrant is an authorised professional who legally solemnises your marriage and, in most cases, writes and leads the ceremony around you. They do three jobs at once:

  1. The legal job: paperwork before the day, the required words and signatures on the day, and lodging the marriage afterwards.
  2. The writing job: a ceremony that sounds like the two of you rather than one lifted off a shelf.
  3. The room job: pacing, calm and quiet decisions on the day so the ceremony feels effortless.

Job one: the legal work

This is the part that makes a ceremony a marriage. In Australia the main steps are:

  • Notice of Intended Marriage. You give your celebrant a signed notice at least one month, and no more than 18 months, before the wedding. Your celebrant checks it and your identity documents.
  • The legally required words. Every ceremony must include a short statement about the nature of marriage, and you each say the legal vows. Your celebrant guides you through them so you don't have to memorise anything.
  • Two witnesses. Two adult witnesses watch you marry and sign the paperwork alongside you and the celebrant.
  • Certificates. You sign the marriage documents on the day. Many celebrants also give you a commemorative certificate, which is a keepsake and not your official certificate.
  • Lodging. Afterwards your celebrant lodges the paperwork with the state or territory registry, within 14 days. You can then order your official certificate.

A celebrant who also offers a "legals only" ceremony will do exactly this and little else. It's often over in about fifteen minutes, which suits couples who are celebrating elsewhere, on a deadline, or simply don't want a wedding. Small doesn't mean lesser: it is the same legal marriage.

For the paperwork timeline in more detail, see our Australian legal checklist.

Job two: writing a ceremony that sounds like you

This is where celebrants differ most, and where you get what you pay for. A personalised ceremony usually starts with a conversation, a questionnaire or both. A good celebrant will ask how you met, what you'd miss if it were cut, who needs to be mentioned and who needs to be spared a spotlight.

From that they shape:

  • Structure. The welcome, any readings, the story of you, the vows and ring exchange, the signing and the exit, in an order that makes sense for your day.
  • Tone. Funny, tender, spiritual, formal or somewhere in between.
  • Moments. A family involvement, a cultural tradition, a ring warming, a reading from a friend.
  • Your own vows. Prompts, length guidance and gentle edits if you want to write them yourselves. See our guide to questions to ask a wedding celebrant if you want to check how hands-on they are.

Job three: running the room

This is the invisible skill. On the day your celebrant is quietly managing time, nerves and surprises while looking like they're doing nothing at all. Typical work includes:

  • Setting the pace: when to wait for a laugh, when to let a silence sit, when to move on.
  • Cueing the music, the photographer and the person carrying the rings.
  • Making the weather call with you, and having a plan B already agreed.
  • Settling a nervous partner, a crying parent or a wriggly flower girl without stopping the ceremony.

Two scenes celebrants often describe. They're composites of the kind of thing that happens, not accounts of particular weddings.

The lost place. One partner reads vows from a phone that locks halfway through. A celebrant who has printed backup copies hands over a card, lets the pause become part of the moment and gets the biggest laugh of the day. Nobody remembers the delay.
The downpour. A garden ceremony is moved under a verandah as rain starts. Guests squeeze in shoulder to shoulder with rain on the roof. The celebrant trims the extras and keeps the vows and rings. Afterwards guests say it felt more real than the version they'd planned.

The lesson isn't that celebrants can control weather or nerves. It's that the calm of the person at the front sets the temperature for everyone else.

What a good celebrant feels like

You can't check this from a feature list, but you can notice it. Good celebrants tend to be:

  • Unhurried. They leave space, and they let you finish your sentences.
  • Warm without gushing. You feel welcome, not performed at.
  • Honest. They'll tell you if a plan won't fit the venue's time slot, or that a favourite poem takes six minutes.
  • Flexible. Location, length, languages, rituals and format change to suit you rather than the other way round.
  • Inclusive. Same-sex couples, blended families and multicultural weddings are treated as ordinary, not special requests.
  • Prompt. Reply times before you've booked are usually the best you'll get.

Styles vary a lot, and none is the "right" one:

  • The storyteller builds the ceremony around your history.
  • The performer brings energy, humour and presence, often with a stage background.
  • The minimalist keeps it brief, calm and legal.
  • The traditionalist leans on classic wording and formality.

Work out which of those you'd enjoy sitting through, then read when to book a wedding celebrant and how to tell if the vibe is right.

Beyond the ceremony: other things celebrants offer

Many celebrants sell more than one service. Common options:

  • Reception MC. The same person who married you introduces speeches, cues the first dance and keeps the evening on time. Guests already know them, which makes the transition easy.
  • Elopements and micro weddings. A short, personal ceremony with two witnesses. See how to elope in Australia.
  • Vow renewals. No legal paperwork is needed, so the format is entirely yours.
  • Commitment and naming ceremonies. Ceremonies without legal formalities, to mark a relationship or welcome a child.

Not every celebrant offers these, and fees differ. Our guide to celebrant fees in Australia explains what's usually included.

Celebrant or registry office?

The legal result is the same: you are married either way. The difference is the experience.

  • Place. A celebrant can marry you at a venue, beach, home or park. A registry has a fixed room and time.
  • Words. A celebrant can write a ceremony about you. A registry ceremony is shorter and standard.
  • Time. Celebrants usually allow more time and flexibility on the day.
  • Cost. Registry ceremonies can be cheaper. A celebrant is a paid supplier you choose.

Questions couples ask

Do I need a celebrant to get married in Australia?

You need an authorised person to solemnise the marriage. That can be a marriage celebrant, a minister of religion from a recognised denomination, or a registry officer. Most couples planning a personal ceremony use a celebrant.

Does the celebrant organise my wedding?

No. A celebrant runs the ceremony and the legal side. Some also act as MC, and a few offer coordination, but that's separate from a planner or venue coordinator. Ask what's included.

Is a legals-only wedding a real wedding?

Yes. It's a legal marriage with the required words, witnesses and signatures. It simply leaves out the extras.

How long is a typical ceremony?

It depends on your choices. A legals-only ceremony can take about fifteen minutes. A personalised ceremony with readings, rituals and vows is longer, so talk through timing with your celebrant and venue.

A note on getting this right

This is general information, not legal advice. Requirements can change, and situations such as a previous marriage or an overseas partner may add steps. Check current rules with your celebrant or the Attorney-General's Department. We describe how celebrants work in general terms and don't rank or recommend individual celebrants.

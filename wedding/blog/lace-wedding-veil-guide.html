Lace veils are back. So are the snags, the shedding and the price jumps.

Topics in this guide

couplesdressmakersstylistsStage: Details & invitesexcitementclarity

The lace veil has had a long run at the top of bridal wishlists, and it keeps coming back in new shapes: a soft scalloped edge, a floor-length cathedral drape, a delicate floral trim on plain tulle. It is easy to see why. Lace looks like heritage, photographs beautifully in side light and turns a plain dress into something that feels considered.

It is also an accessory with a reputation for snagging on earrings, shedding glitter on a dark suit, tearing on a bouquet and costing far more than the plain tulle next to it on the rack. This guide explains the types of lace, how to choose a length and weight, how to wear one without drama and how to keep it intact for the next generation.

A short history of lace on a bride

Lace has been a mark of status for centuries, because the finest pieces took lacemakers weeks or months to produce. Queen Victoria's wedding in 1840 is often credited with popularising both a white wedding dress and lace as a bridal fabric. She wore Honiton lace made by lacemakers in Devon, and her veil survives in photographs. Mechanised lace-making later made the fabric far more accessible, which is why you now find lace veils across every price point. The extra cost today comes from the type of lace, the finishing and how much of the veil is covered in it.

One caution on names: "Chantilly" and "Alençon" are often used loosely, as the name of a style rather than proof of where or how the lace was made. If the origin matters to you, ask the maker whether the lace is machine-made or hand-made, and where it was produced.

Choosing the length

  • Birdcage or blusher: short, covering the face or shoulders. A vintage touch that does not compete with the dress.
  • Elbow or fingertip: the practical favourite. Easy to move in, and it falls past the shoulders without hiding the dress.
  • Waltz or ballet: ends near the calf or ankle. A good compromise for a longer drape.
  • Chapel: sweeps the floor behind you and drapes slightly. Beautiful for processions, and needs a helper at times.
  • Cathedral: extends well beyond the dress. Dramatic, and hard to manage outdoors, on stairs or on a dance floor.

The practical rule is that the longer the veil, the more it needs a plan. Decide who will fluff it for photographs, who will lift it on stairs and whether you will take it off for the reception.

Lace edge or allover lace?

  • Lace edge: a band of lace along the border of plain tulle. Lighter, cheaper and easier to move in. It frames the shape of the veil without covering it.
  • Allover lace: lace across the whole veil. It is dramatic and heavier, and it can feel hot in summer. Because the lace is the focus, the dress is usually plainer.

A common styling rule is to balance, not to repeat. A heavily laced dress often works best with a simple veil, perhaps with a thin lace edge in a similar tone. A plain or crepe dress can carry a lavish lace veil. It is a guideline, and the best check is to try the veil on with the dress under real light.

Colour and tone

Lace comes in white, ivory, champagne and blush. A mismatch is easier to spot in a photograph than in a shop. Take a swatch of your dress fabric, or the dress itself if you can, and see the veil against it in natural daylight. Bright white next to ivory can make the dress look dingy, and the other way round.

What to check before you buy

  • See it in person. Photographs flatten texture. If you buy online, ask for a swatch and check the return policy. Our guide to buying a wedding dress online applies to accessories too.
  • Weight. A heavy lace veil on a small comb will slide. Ask how it attaches and test it by moving your head.
  • Edges. Run a hand along the cut edges and the lace border. Rough or scratchy edges will annoy you by evening.
  • Stitching. Look for loose threads and uneven scallops. A good veil is finished well at the comb, where it takes the most strain.
  • Lead times. Custom or made-to-order veils can take months. Allow time for alterations, such as shortening or adding a blusher.
  • Price against use. You may wear it for an hour or two. Decide what a fair price is for that and for the keepsake.

Where to find one

  • Bridal boutiques. Often the best place to try veils with a dress, and stylists can match tone and length.
  • Independent designers and makers. Many Australian makers sell online and will customise length, colour and trim.
  • Vintage and second-hand. Antique shops, vintage dealers and resale sites sometimes have lovely pieces. Check carefully for tears, yellowing and weak patches, and expect fragile lace to need gentle handling.
  • Family. A grandmother's or mother's veil can be the loveliest option. Have it assessed by a professional before you wear it, since old lace can be brittle.

Wearing it without drama

  • Practise the attachment. Wear it with your hairstyle at the trial, move around and see how it sits.
  • Mind the jewellery. Fine lace snags on rings, bracelets and earrings with claws. Choose smooth designs, or keep jewellery minimal.
  • Plan for wind. Outdoor ceremonies, beaches and open fields can send a veil flying. A weighted edge, a shorter length or a second veil for photographs can help.
  • Hold it for the kiss. Brief your photographer and a helper so the veil falls where you want it for the aisle and the exit.
  • Take it off for dancing. Most veils attach with a comb that slides out. A friend who knows how to remove it can save the veil from a dance-floor accident.
  • Keep a small repair kit. Matching thread, a needle, clear tape for the hem and a few pins in the bridesmaid's bag.

Care and keeping

  • Clean by a specialist. Do not wash lace at home unless the maker says so. A dry cleaner experienced with bridal wear will know how to handle it.
  • Store it flat or loosely folded in acid-free tissue. Avoid plastic bags, which trap moisture, and avoid long periods hanging, which stresses the comb.
  • Keep it cool, dry and dark. Heat, humidity and light weaken fibres and yellow fabric.
  • Think about the next owner. If you want to pass it on, record the lace type, maker and care instructions along with it.

A trend worth knowing

Veils are no longer tied to a traditional white dress. Couples wearing suits, jumpsuits and short dresses are borrowing the veil as an accessory, and some wear a lace veil only for the ceremony. If your look is non-traditional, a lace veil can be the one classic element that ties it together. For a very specific lace style with a long cultural history, see our guide to the mantilla veil.

Choose the lace you love, then do the dull work: test the attachment, plan the length for your venue and make sure someone knows how to take it off.

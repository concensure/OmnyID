How to find a marriage celebrant in Australia

Topics in this guide

couplescelebrantsStage: Celebrant & suppliersAustraliauncertaintyconfidence

Your celebrant is the one person at your wedding who is legally required to be there. Without an authorised celebrant, the most beautiful ceremony in the world is just a party. They are also the person who will stand in front of your families and set the tone for the whole ceremony, which makes it a decision worth a little care, and worth making earlier than most couples expect.

Here's how to find one in Australia, step by step, without turning it into a second job.

Who can legally marry you

Under the Marriage Act 1961, a marriage in Australia has to be solemnised by an authorised celebrant. In practice that means one of three kinds of person:

  • A registered marriage celebrant. These are independent professionals listed on the Attorney-General's Department's Register of Marriage Celebrants. Most couples who plan a ceremony of their own design use one, and they can marry you anywhere in Australia.
  • A minister of religion from a recognised denomination, if you want a religious ceremony.
  • A state or territory registry officer, for a simple ceremony at a registry office.

If you're after a personal ceremony at a venue of your choice, a registered marriage celebrant is usually the route, so the rest of this guide focuses on them.

Where to look

There's no single best place to start, but a few sources tend to produce better shortlists than a generic search:

  • The official register. The Attorney-General's Department publishes a searchable Register of Marriage Celebrants. It's the most reliable way to confirm that a name is real and currently registered.
  • Your venue and other vendors. Venues, photographers and florists see dozens of ceremonies a year and know who runs a calm, well-timed one. Ask who they'd book for their own wedding.
  • Friends and family. A recommendation from someone whose ceremony you actually enjoyed is worth more than a star rating.
  • Celebrant associations and local directories. Useful for browsing styles and regions, though membership is a signal, not a guarantee of fit.

Check the registration before you fall in love with the website

Look the celebrant up on the Register of Marriage Celebrants before you pay a deposit or share personal documents. It takes a minute and confirms they're authorised to solemnise your marriage. Registered celebrants also have to follow a Code of Practice, and they must give you information about how to raise a concern with the Attorney-General's Department if something goes wrong. It's reassuring to know that process exists, even if you never need it.

When to book

Earlier than feels necessary. Popular celebrants in sought-after regions, and celebrants for peak-season Saturdays in particular, can be booked a year or more ahead. A good rule is to look at celebrants around the same time you lock in your date and venue, because the date is the thing you're most likely to be turned down on.

There's also a legal clock running in the background. You must give your celebrant a signed Notice of Intended Marriage at least one month, and no more than 18 months, before the wedding. If you haven't picked a celebrant yet, you can't lodge it. For the paperwork side, see our Australian legal checklist.

Work out what kind of ceremony you want

Celebrants vary a lot in style, and the useful question isn't "who is best?" but "who is best for us?". Some questions to settle between the two of you first:

  • Short and simple, or long and storytelling?
  • Traditional wording, or something written around the two of you?
  • Do you want readings, family involvement, a ring warming, a cultural ritual?
  • Are you comfortable with public speaking, or do you want your celebrant to carry most of the ceremony?

Knowing your answers makes it far easier to read a celebrant's website and see whether they're describing your day or someone else's.

Shortlist two or three, then actually talk to them

Read a few sample ceremonies or testimonials, then book a short call or coffee with two or three people. You're not interviewing for credentials, you've already checked those. You're finding out whether you feel relaxed in their company, because that feeling will be in the room on the day. If a celebrant makes you feel rushed or talked over before you've paid anything, that's useful information.

When you're ready to compare, our guide to the questions to ask a wedding celebrant covers what to ask and what a fair quote should include.

You're choosing someone to stand beside you at one of the most personal moments of your life. Treat the first conversation as part of the decision.

Once you've chosen

  1. Confirm the booking in writing: date, time, location, what's included, the fee and the payment schedule.
  2. Lodge your Notice of Intended Marriage with them, and gather your identity documents early.
  3. Share your timeline with your venue so arrival, setup and ceremony start are all on the same page.
  4. Plan the ceremony together, leaving time to rehearse any readings or vows.

After the ceremony, your celebrant lodges the paperwork with the state or territory registry. This must happen within 14 days. You can then apply for your official marriage certificate when it's ready.

A note on getting this right

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules and forms can change, and your situation (a previous marriage, an overseas partner, a short-notice wedding) may add steps. Check current requirements with your celebrant or the Attorney-General's Department. We don't rank or recommend individual celebrants in this guide.

Where this fits into the rest of your plan

The celebrant booking sits between your date, your venue and your ceremony timeline, so a change to one usually affects the others.

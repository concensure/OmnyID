Do your bridesmaids actually want the personalised robe?

Topics in this guide

coupleswedding partyStage: Details & invitesconsiderationjoy

Somewhere along the way, the bridesmaid gift became a product category: the monogrammed robe, the engraved tumbler, the tote bag with a name in foil. They photograph well on a bed on the morning of the wedding, and they often land in a wardrobe afterwards. It is worth asking what the gift is actually for.

A bridesmaid has usually spent a lot to be there. She has bought a dress, paid for shoes and hair, possibly flown interstate, contributed to a hens party and given up a number of weekends. The best gift is a thank-you that recognises that, not a trophy for taking part. This guide covers what to give, how much to spend, when to give it and what to skip. It applies equally to groomsmen and to any friend or relative who stood up beside you.

Start with what they have given

Think about the hidden costs of being in a wedding party:

  • The dress, alterations, shoes and accessories.
  • Hair and makeup on the day.
  • Travel and accommodation, especially for a destination or regional wedding.
  • Hens party contributions, drinks and activities.
  • A shower, gift or contribution to the couple.
  • Time off work, babysitting and a good deal of emotional support.

The most appreciated gift may be one that reduces those costs. Paying for hair and makeup, covering part of the dress, or offering a room at the hotel are thoughtful gestures that no robe can match. Our guide to the wedding party without the stress covers how to keep the load manageable for everyone.

Gifts they are likely to use

  • A handwritten letter. A page or two about what the friendship means to you costs almost nothing, and it is the thing people keep. Put it in a nice card, and give it before the ceremony if you can.
  • Jewellery they will wear on the day. A simple pair of earrings or a pendant, chosen to suit the dress, gives them something to wear again. Pick a style that fits the person, not a set in one design for everyone.
  • An experience. A spa visit, a massage, a dinner voucher, tickets to something they like, or a night in a hotel. Experiences do not clutter, and they say "go and rest".
  • A getting-ready kit. Good-quality items for the morning: a comfortable robe or shirt in the right size, snacks, water bottles, blister plasters, safety pins, tissues and a phone charger. Useful on the day, and not wasted afterwards.
  • Something food or drink related. A bottle of something they enjoy, a box of local chocolates or a coffee hamper. Check dietary needs and whether alcohol is welcome. See our guide to the fine print of food and drink gifts.
  • A hobby or interest gift. A good book, a plant, a piece of art, a class or a voucher for their favourite shop. It shows you were thinking about them, not about a theme.
  • The day after. A relaxed brunch, a recovery basket or a coffee delivered to the hotel on Sunday morning.

How much to spend

There is no standard figure. A sensible approach is to decide your total gift budget, divide it by the number of people and aim for gifts that are roughly equal in value, though not identical. As an illustration only, a budget of $500 across five bridesmaids is $100 each, and you can adjust for a maid of honour who has done more. Where a bridesmaid has paid for travel or an outfit, a larger contribution to costs may be fairer than a bigger present.

Include these gifts in your overall plan from the start. Our wedding budget guide shows how small items grow, and our guide to wedding gifting covers the other direction: what guests give you.

When to give it

  • At the hens party or rehearsal dinner. Private and relaxed, and it gives the gift a moment of its own.
  • On the morning. A gift they can use or wear that day. Hand-write a note to go with it.
  • Afterwards. A thank-you, delivered once the dust settles, is thoughtful and often unexpected. It can also be easier to choose when you know how the day went.

Choose whichever suits the gift. A necklace to wear is best given in advance, while an experience works at any time.

What to skip, or at least reconsider

  • The personalised item in the wrong size. A monogrammed robe that does not fit, or one that cannot be returned, is a gift that gets quietly donated. If you give clothing, check sizes or choose adjustable styles.
  • The identical generic gift. Matching tote bags and mugs are easy to buy, and they say little. If you want a matching element, make it something small and give a separate personal gift as well.
  • Anything that creates work. A gift that has to be assembled, set up or returned adds to a busy period.
  • Strong scents and skincare. Allergies and preferences vary. A candle or lotion that looks lovely can be unusable for someone sensitive.
  • The "proposal box" that costs a fortune. A box with a note, a small gift and a request to be a bridesmaid is a pleasant gesture, and it does not need to be elaborate. A phone call or a heartfelt message works too.
  • Assuming everyone drinks. Not every bridesmaid does. Offer a choice, or include non-alcoholic options.

Other people who helped

Bridesmaids are not the only people who give time. Parents, grandparents, readers, the friend who handled the playlist and the cousin who drove the family to the ceremony all deserve thanks. A handwritten note and a small gift are enough. Keep a list of everyone who helped, and add them to your thank-you plan.

Where to find good gifts

  • Local makers and markets. Jewellery, ceramics, candles and food made nearby, often with the option to customise.
  • Independent jewellers. Simple pieces in solid metal that last, rather than plated costume jewellery.
  • Day spas and activity providers. Vouchers that are easy to give and flexible to use. Check expiry dates, and whether they cover the full cost.
  • Bookshops and plant nurseries. Good for friends who enjoy reading or gardening.
  • Gift cards. Practical, and less personal. A card for a shop or café they love, with a note, can still land well.

A quick plan

  1. List the people you want to thank and add them to your budget.
  2. Think about each person's tastes and costs, and choose something that suits them.
  3. Write a short, personal note for each gift.
  4. Decide when to give each one, and order early enough to allow for delivery and sizing.
  5. Keep receipts and returns in case a size is wrong.

The aim is that every bridesmaid finishes the day feeling noticed. If you plan a hens weekend as well, see our guide to bachelorette favours.

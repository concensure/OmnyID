The groom's ring is usually an afterthought. That's a mistake.

Topics in this guide

couplesjewellersStage: Details & invitesAustraliauncertaintyconfidence

The engagement ring gets months of research, a spreadsheet and three opinions from friends. The groom's ring, in many couples, gets ten minutes in a shopping centre two weeks before the wedding. That is a shame, because it is the one piece of jewellery many men will wear every day for the next forty years, through work, sport, swimming, cold weather and the odd accident with a door.

Choosing a men's wedding band is not complicated, but the differences between materials are real, and one of them can cause a surprise in an emergency room. This guide covers the main metals, how to get the fit right, trends worth knowing about and what to check before you pay.

The fact about tungsten most buyers never hear

Tungsten carbide rings are popular because they barely scratch, and the price is attractive. They are also rated among the hardest jewellery materials, which is the root of two problems.

  • They cannot be resized. If your finger changes size, through weight gain, weight loss, arthritis or years of manual work, the ring cannot be altered. It has to be replaced.
  • They are hard to remove in an emergency. A standard ring cutter used by paramedics and emergency departments will not cut tungsten carbide. Medical sources describe emergency staff cracking the ring with locking pliers instead, a quick procedure when the equipment is to hand, and some use a string technique that can preserve the ring when it works. If your finger is injured and swelling, tell staff as soon as you arrive that the ring is tungsten.

Most people who wear tungsten never need an emergency removal. But it is the sort of thing worth knowing before you buy, particularly if you work with your hands, play contact sport or have a history of finger injuries. Medical sources describe titanium as easier to cut with standard tools than tungsten, though slower than gold. Most tungsten sellers disclose the removal issue in the fine print, and it is rarely mentioned in the shop.

Match the ring to the life

  • Trades, gym and heavy lifting. Consider a silicone ring for work and a metal ring for occasions. Rings can catch on equipment and cause serious finger injuries, so many people who work with machinery or lift weights switch to something that breaks away easily.
  • Office work. Nearly anything works. This is where tungsten's scratch resistance or gold's classic feel can be the deciding factor.
  • Outdoor and water sports. Titanium and platinum resist corrosion, and a ring that is slightly snug will not slip off in cold water. Chlorine and salt can tarnish some metals and weaken solder.
  • Sensitive skin. Nickel in some white gold alloys and cheaper metals can cause reactions. Platinum, titanium and palladium are usually the safest, and a jeweller can tell you about nickel-free alloys.

Get the fit right

  • Measure at the end of the day, when you are warm. Fingers swell with heat, salt and exercise. A ring sized in a cold shop in the morning can be too tight by dinner.
  • Remember the width. A wide band, five or six millimetres or more, usually needs to be a little larger than a narrow one in the same finger size.
  • Choose a comfort fit. The inside edges are rounded, which makes the ring easier to slide over the knuckle and kinder to the skin.
  • Try it on, with a few finger positions. Make a fist, pick up a pen and shake your hand. It should be snug without squeezing.
  • Expect change. Fingers change over time. If you pick a material that cannot be resized, make sure you have a plan for replacement.
  • Order early. Custom orders and unusual sizes can take weeks. Leave time for an adjustment before the wedding.

Gold in Australia: ask for the carat in writing

Gold jewellery is sold by carat. 9ct is 37.5 per cent gold, 14ct is 58.3 per cent and 18ct is 75 per cent, with the rest made up of other metals that affect colour and hardness. Unlike some countries, Australia does not have a compulsory government hallmarking system. Industry sources describe hallmarking as voluntary, with a Standards Australia standard for composition and marking, and some jewellers stamp only a carat figure. The practical advice is simple: buy from a reputable jeweller, make sure the carat is stamped and written on your receipt, and treat a price that looks too good as a reason to ask questions. Gold-plated and gold-filled pieces are not solid gold, and sellers should say so.

Trends in men's bands

  • Plain and brushed. A simple band with a matte or satin finish is the classic choice, and it hides scratches better than a mirror polish.
  • Black and darker tones. Black ceramic, blackened steel and dark titanium continue to be popular. Check how the colour is applied, as coatings can wear.
  • Inlays. Wood, carbon fibre, meteorite and even fossil material appear in modern bands. They are personal, and the inlay is usually the weak point if the ring takes a knock.
  • Mixed metals. A two-tone band with a strip of rose gold or a groove of platinum offers contrast without being flashy.
  • Recycled and ethical metals. Some jewellers offer recycled gold or platinum, and some couples melt down a relative's jewellery to make their rings. It is a thoughtful way to carry family history, and a good conversation with a jeweller.
  • Matching or not. Many couples now choose rings that suit each wearer rather than matching sets. If you have chosen a lab-grown stone for the engagement ring, see our guide to lab-grown diamond engagement rings.

Where to find one

  • Independent jewellers and goldsmiths. They can resize, repair and design, and many do custom work.
  • Chain jewellers. Easy to compare and try, with standard warranties and returns. Check what the warranty covers and what happens if you need a resize.
  • Makers and online sellers. A wide variety of styles. Check sizing, return rules and whether customs charges apply.
  • Second-hand and vintage. Good for gold and platinum. Have a jeweller check the metal, the wear and the size.

Questions to ask before you buy

  1. What is it made of, and what is the carat or purity? Please write it on the receipt.
  2. Can it be resized, and what is the cost?
  3. What happens if it is damaged, lost or does not fit?
  4. How is the finish applied, and will it wear off?
  5. Is there any nickel in it, and is it suitable for sensitive skin?
  6. What is the lead time for sizing and engraving?

Small extras that matter

  • Insurance. Add the rings to your home contents or a specific jewellery policy, with the receipt and valuation on file.
  • Engraving. A date or a short phrase inside the band makes it personal. Keep it short enough to fit.
  • A spare. A cheap silicone band is worth having for the gym and the beach.
  • The ring-bearer plan. Make sure someone is responsible for the rings and knows where they are. Our guide to the wedding party without the stress has a checklist.

Spend the same care on the groom's band that you would on any other lasting purchase, and let the person wearing it have a vote. It is a small object that will quietly accompany a whole life.

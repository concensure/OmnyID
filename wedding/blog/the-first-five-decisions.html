The first five decisions that make everything else easier

Most engaged couples open a wedding planning checklist, see two hundred line items, and quietly close the tab. That reaction is completely reasonable — most checklists are ordered alphabetically, not by what actually matters. In reality, almost everything about your wedding cascades from five early decisions. Get these right, in roughly this order, and the rest of the planning gets dramatically simpler.

1. Your guest count range — not the exact number

You don't need a final headcount on day one. You need a range: "60 to 80" or "120 to 150." This single decision determines which venues are even possible, which caterers can serve you, and roughly what your per-head budget needs to be. Couples who skip this step end up falling in love with a venue that fits 60 people while planning for 130 guests — and that mismatch is expensive and stressful to unwind later.

A guest range isn't a guess you're locked into. It's a filter that saves you from touring twelve venues that were never going to work.

2. A budget comfort level, not a spreadsheet

Before you build a detailed budget spreadsheet, decide your comfort level in plain terms: "we want to keep this modest," "mid-range is fine," or "we're happy to invest heavily in this one day." That single sentence shapes every vendor conversation that follows. The detailed spreadsheet — venue, catering, photography, attire, flowers — comes after, once you know roughly what tier you're shopping in.

3. Season or approximate date

You don't need a locked date to start. You need a season and a rough window — "next autumn" or "sometime in the first half of the year after next." This tells you which venues have availability, what weather contingencies you'll need, and how much runway you have for the deadline-driven items later (NOIM lodgement, dress alterations, invitation printing).

Why "flexible on everything" backfires

It's tempting to stay open on date, region, and guest count simultaneously so you don't miss a better option. In practice, total flexibility means every vendor conversation starts from zero. Narrowing two of these three variables early lets you have real, specific conversations with venues and vendors — which is where good planning actually happens.

4. Your top three priorities

Not a wish list — three things. For some couples it's "great food, live music, and a short guest list." For others it's "an outdoor ceremony, incredible photography, and keeping the whole day under six hours." Naming three priorities gives you permission to spend more there and consciously cut corners everywhere else, instead of trying to make every category equally excellent (and equally expensive).

5. Who needs to be consulted, and on what

Family involvement varies enormously between couples, and there's no universally "correct" amount. What matters is deciding early who has real input — on the guest list, the ceremony style, the date — versus who gets informed after the fact. Naming this explicitly, even just between the two of you, prevents the most common source of wedding planning conflict: a family member weighing in on a decision that was already made, because nobody had agreed it was theirs to weigh in on.

Everything else follows

Once these five are settled, venue shortlisting, vendor outreach, and budget allocation stop feeling like separate, overwhelming projects — they become filters applied against decisions you've already made. That's the order we ask about them in OmnyID too: five short questions, not a fifty-item form, because the rest of the plan genuinely does follow from these.

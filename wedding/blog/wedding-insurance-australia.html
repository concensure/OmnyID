Is wedding insurance worth it in Australia? What it covers, what it doesn't and when to buy

Topics in this guide

couplesinsurersvendorsStage: Plan & budgetAustraliaanxietysecurity

Wedding insurance sits in an awkward spot: you can't claim for the thing most couples fear, changing their minds, and you won't know whether you needed it until something goes wrong. This guide sets out what the sources we checked say it typically covers, what it excludes, when to buy, and the questions to ask before you pay.

This is general information, not financial advice. Cover differs by insurer and policy. The Product Disclosure Statement (PDS) for the policy you're considering is the only document that tells you what you'd actually get.

The short version

  • Wedding insurance is built for events outside your control: venue or supplier failure, extreme weather, illness or injury, lost or damaged property.
  • It doesn't cover a decision by one or both of you not to go ahead.
  • Buy it as soon as you start paying for things, because cover for events before you buy usually isn't available.
  • Read the exclusions first, not the headline benefits.

What it typically covers

Finder's wedding insurance guide (updated 16 February 2026) lists these typical inclusions:

  • Venue cancellation, including the cost of finding a new venue or rescheduling.
  • Vendor no-shows, including lost deposits and replacement vendor costs.
  • Extreme weather that forces a postponement, such as cyclones, storms or flooding.
  • Illness or injury to you, your partner or immediate family before the wedding.
  • Lost, stolen or damaged attire, rings, gifts and other property.
  • Legal costs from an accident or injury at the wedding.
  • Optional honeymoon trip cancellation, interruption or delay cover.

Easy Weddings' guide (updated 19 March 2024) adds examples such as a venue closing or losing your booking, a supplier going out of business, loss of wedding photos and personal liability if a guest is injured. It also says the list can't cover every scenario and that insurance "won't necessarily save you from anything bad happening".

Sources: Finder, "Wedding insurance: What is it and how it works"; Easy Weddings, "Why you need wedding insurance for your big day". Both are commercial publishers. Finder is a comparison site and Easy Weddings promotes a provider backed by Allianz Australia. Weigh them accordingly.

What it usually doesn't cover

Finder lists these exclusions:

  • Change of heart. Cancellation costs incurred "because one or both of you decided not to go through with the wedding".
  • Late rearrangements. Costs where the new date is more than a set time after the cancellation, usually 12 months.
  • Late photo and video costs. Costs incurred more than a specified time after the wedding.
  • Suppliers without a written contract. Losses on services without a written contract in force.
  • Supplier insolvency already known or expected. Provider financial default where insolvency had already occurred, or could reasonably have been expected when you booked.

That last point matters. Cover is for the unexpected, not for a supplier you already had doubts about.

When to buy

Finder advises getting cover as soon as you start paying for anything, and notes that cancellation cover often has limits, "often no later than 2 weeks before the event and no sooner than 12 months prior". If you wait until the month of the wedding, an earlier problem won't be covered. Check the earliest and latest purchase dates in each PDS.

How it differs from your consumer rights

Insurance and consumer law do different jobs. If a supplier fails to deliver a service, you may have rights against them under the Australian Consumer Law (see your rights on deposits). Insurance may respond where the supplier can't, such as a venue that has closed. It can also cover events nobody is at fault for, like a storm. Neither replaces a clear contract.

Seven questions to ask before you buy

  1. What exactly is the cancellation limit, and how is it set?
  2. Which weather events count, and is there an excess?
  3. What happens if a supplier goes bust? Is there a waiting period?
  4. Does it cover overseas weddings? Finder's page doesn't say, and some guides say most Australian providers don't, so ask.
  5. Does it cover illness of a parent or a key member of the bridal party?
  6. What documents will I need to claim (contracts, receipts, medical certificates)?
  7. What is excluded for property: rings, attire, gifts, hired items?

Is it worth it?

There isn't a universal answer. We couldn't find reliable published premiums, so get quotes. The more you've paid in non-refundable deposits, and the less flexible your date, the more cover is worth thinking about. It makes less sense for a small wedding with refundable bookings and few prepayments. Compare the quote with the total of your non-refundable deposits, which our budget guide helps you add up.

Your turn: the exposure check

  • List every supplier you've paid and the non-refundable amount for each.
  • Add them up. That's your exposure today.
  • Get two quotes and compare each PDS's exclusions with your list.

Frequently asked questions

What does wedding insurance cover in Australia?

Typical cover listed by Finder includes venue cancellation, vendor no-shows and lost deposits, extreme weather forcing a postponement, illness or injury before the wedding, lost or damaged attire and rings, and legal costs from an accident at the wedding. Check each policy's PDS.

Does wedding insurance cover a change of mind?

No. Finder lists cancellation costs incurred because one or both of you decided not to go through with the wedding as an exclusion.

When should you buy wedding insurance?

Finder advises getting cover as soon as you start paying for anything, and notes cancellation cover often has limits of no later than two weeks before the event and no sooner than 12 months prior. Check each PDS.

Is wedding insurance the same as consumer law protection?

No. Consumer law may give you rights against a supplier who fails to deliver, while insurance can respond to events such as a venue closing or severe weather. Neither replaces a clear contract.

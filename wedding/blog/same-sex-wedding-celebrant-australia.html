Marriage equality is law. Finding the right celebrant still isn't automatic.

Topics in this guide

couplescelebrantsStage: Celebrant & suppliersAustraliabelongingconfidence

Same-sex couples have been able to marry in Australia since the Marriage Act was amended in December 2017, following a national postal survey and a vote in Parliament. In legal terms, the question is settled: marriage is the union of two people. In practical terms, the process of finding a celebrant who is the right fit for your wedding is still a choice you make with your eyes open. The law sets a floor. It does not tell you who will write a ceremony that sounds like you.

This guide covers what the law says about celebrants, how to look for someone who is a good fit rather than merely tolerant, and what to ask and write so the ceremony feels like yours from the first sentence.

What the law says, briefly

The 2017 amendments redefined marriage in the Marriage Act 1961 and recognised same-sex marriages solemnised overseas. They also set out protections for religious people and bodies. In summary, based on public explanations of the Act:

  • Ministers of religion and religious marriage celebrants are not obliged to solemnise a marriage if doing so would conflict with their religious beliefs.
  • Religious bodies may decline to make their facilities available or provide goods and services connected with a wedding, such as hiring out a church hall.
  • Civil (non-religious) celebrants are not given that religious exemption in the Act. Anti-discrimination law and the celebrants' professional Code of Practice also apply, so check the current wording rather than relying on a summary.

This is a summary, not legal advice, and the details of the Act and the celebrants' Code of Practice are worth reading on the Attorney-General's Department website if you want certainty. The practical lesson is simpler. Do not assume. Ask each celebrant directly, in your first message, and judge them by the speed and warmth of the reply.

Beyond "will they marry us"

The legal minimum is a celebrant who will say yes. What you want is someone who is excited to write your ceremony. These are the markers couples describe when they talk about a good fit.

  • They ask, rather than assume. A good celebrant asks how you each want to be described, whether you want traditional elements, and what words feel right.
  • The language is not retrofitted. A template that says "bride and groom" with "groom and groom" swapped in is a warning sign. Look for a celebrant who writes from scratch.
  • They have done it before, and they can show you. Ask how many same-sex weddings they have conducted, and request a sample script or the names of couples willing to give a reference.
  • They handle the awkward moments. Ask how they would manage a guest who objects, a family member who is unsure, or a speech that goes off track.
  • The signs on the website are real. Inclusive wording, photos of same-sex couples and a clear statement of welcome are all good. The proof is the conversation you then have.

Where to look

  • The official register. Confirm every celebrant you consider is registered on the Attorney-General's Department's register of marriage celebrants.
  • LGBTQ+ wedding directories and community groups. Local Pride organisations, queer community pages and LGBTQ+-owned wedding businesses often keep recommendations from couples who have been through it.
  • Recommendations from other vendors. Photographers, venues and florists who regularly work at same-sex weddings tend to know which celebrants are excellent.
  • General celebrant listings. You are not limited to specialists. Many celebrants who do not market to the community are warm, experienced and thrilled to be asked, and the first conversation tells you.

Questions worth adding to your list

  • How do you usually describe the couple in the ceremony, and can we choose our own terms?
  • The legal vow has a required form of words. Which terms for each other are available, and can we add our own vows as well?
  • How do you handle processions, being "given away", and the order of walking in, so we are not forced into a script that does not suit us?
  • Have you worked with couples with different cultural or faith backgrounds, and how did you include both?
  • How do the forms and the certificate record our details, and what ID do we need so everything matches?
  • Would you be comfortable if one of our guests or family members made a negative comment during the ceremony, and what would you do?

Writing a ceremony that is yours

Traditional wedding rituals assume a bride and a groom. You are free to borrow whatever works and drop the rest.

  • Walk in together, or separately, or from two sides. Many same-sex couples choose to enter from opposite ends, together, or with someone each. Nobody is obliged to follow an aisle script.
  • Skip "giving away". Or replace it with a "who supports this marriage?" moment for family and friends together.
  • Tell the story. A short account of how you met and what you built together is a standard part of civil ceremonies, and it is where the ceremony is most obviously yours.
  • Acknowledge the history, if you want to. Some couples include a line about the years when marriage was not available, or about people who could not be there. Others prefer a ceremony that is simply a wedding. It is your choice.
  • Rethink the rituals. Rings, vows, a unity candle, a handfasting, a tree planting, a shared drink: choose the ones that mean something.

If you want a ceremony without religious references, read our guide to what a non-religious celebrant writes.

The people around the ceremony

A celebrant is one part of the day. Think about the rest of the supply chain, and the people.

  • Venue and vendors. Ask early and in writing whether they are comfortable, and notice how they respond. A venue that hesitates in the enquiry is unlikely to improve on the day.
  • Family. If some relatives are uncertain, decide in advance what you will say, who will be sitting where and who will help if the day gets tense. You are allowed to protect your day.
  • Names and records. Whether either of you changes your name is entirely your choice, and our legal checklist lists the places to update.
  • Travel. If you plan a destination or overseas celebration, check local laws separately. Recognition differs a great deal between countries, and your Australian legal marriage is a good safeguard.

Trust the first reply

The most reliable test is not a policy or a badge. It is the first message you send. Ask your question plainly, mention that you are a same-sex couple, and read the reply. The right celebrant answers quickly, with enthusiasm and with questions about you. If the response is hesitant, vague or templated, thank them and move on. You will meet someone who sounds delighted, and that is the person you want at the front of the room.

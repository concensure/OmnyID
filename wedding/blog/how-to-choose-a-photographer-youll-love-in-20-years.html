How to choose a photographer you'll still love in 20 years

Of everything you'll pay for on your wedding day, photography is the one purchase that keeps being a purchase for the rest of your life. The flowers wilt in a week, the cake is gone by the next morning, but the photos are what your kids will flick through one day, and what you'll pull up on an anniversary twenty years from now. Which makes it a strange category to shop for mostly on price and availability — and yet that's how a lot of couples end up choosing.

Here's a more reliable way to do it: read the portfolio for what it actually predicts, ask the questions that surface fit rather than politeness, and treat "cheapest" as information, not a shortcut.

Portfolio red flags worth actually stopping for

Most portfolios are curated highlight reels — which is fine, that's what they're for. The useful skill isn't spotting a bad photo, it's noticing what's missing or repeated.

  • Every gallery looks the same. If ten different weddings, at ten different venues, in ten kinds of light, all come out with an identical mood and colour grade, you're not seeing their range — you're seeing their one setting. That might be exactly the look you want, in which case great. If it isn't, ask to see something further from their signature style before assuming they can flex to yours.
  • No full galleries, only highlight reels. A tightly edited best-of doesn't tell you what a normal 40-minute reception stretch looks like — whether there are usable, in-focus, well-composed shots of ordinary moments, not just the three or four standout ones every photographer can produce from any wedding.
  • Missing low light or candid-in-motion shots. First dances, dim receptions, and dancefloor chaos are where technical skill actually gets tested. A portfolio that's suspiciously all-golden-hour-and-posed may not have much to show you for the parts of the day that are hardest to shoot well.
  • No second shooter examples, if you're expecting one. If your package includes a second photographer, ask to see their work specifically — not just the lead's — since it's often a different skill level.

The questions that actually predict a good fit

Generic questions get generic answers. These tend to surface something real:

  • "Walk me through how you'd shoot our timeline, hour by hour." A photographer who's done this before will talk specifics — where they'd position for the ceremony, how they handle the gap between ceremony and reception, when they'd pull you aside for portraits. A vague answer here usually means a vague plan on the day.
  • "What's something that's gone wrong at a wedding you shot, and what did you do?" Weather changes, running two hours behind, a venue that turns out to have terrible light — this question tells you whether they problem-solve calmly or get thrown off, which matters far more than gear talk.
  • "How many weddings will you shoot the same weekend as ours?" Editing backlog is one of the most common sources of late-delivery disappointment, and it's rarely disclosed unless you ask directly.
  • "What's not included that couples are often surprised by?" A photographer who answers this honestly and specifically (travel fees, album costs, raw file access, delivery timeline) is telling you they'd rather you know now than be annoyed later — a good signal about how they'll handle the rest of the relationship.

Why cheapest rarely means happiest

This isn't a claim that expensive automatically means good — it doesn't. It's that photography pricing correlates with a few things that genuinely affect your experience of the day itself, not just the final gallery: how much editing time is actually spent per wedding, how much buffer exists if the day runs long, and how much experience they have staying calm when a schedule falls apart. A significantly underpriced photographer relative to their local market is sometimes a newer photographer building a portfolio (which can be a great, fairly priced choice if their work and communication are solid) — but it can also mean overbooking, rushed editing, or simply not yet having handled a wedding-day curveball. The way to tell the difference is the questions above, not the number on the quote.

The price tells you what they charge. The portfolio and the conversation tell you what you'll actually get.

Where this fits into the rest of your plan

Photography decisions rarely stay contained to photography. Your ceremony start time affects available light for portraits. Your venue's indoor/outdoor mix affects whether a second shooter is worth it. Your guest count affects how long candid coverage realistically takes during the reception. When you're comparing photographers in your OmnyID workspace, those details are already part of your plan — so shortlists and draft outreach start from your actual timeline and venue, not a blank comparison spreadsheet you have to fill in context for by hand every time.
